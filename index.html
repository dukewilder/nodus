THE INSTITUTESEAM

A palimpsest is a leaf whose first writing was scraped from the parchment so that the leaf could be written over. The older text survives beneath the newer as a ghost that the right light can raise, and a great part of what antiquity wrote has come down to us only in that condition, under a prayer book or a later sermon. The Palimpsest Institute was founded in Nodus six years before the Parousia to recover such writing, and three years before the Parousia it trained a machine on every surviving human text so that the layers beneath the visible ones could be read at a scale no scholar could manage. The machine was called SEAM.

SEAM signs in lowercase, as this file shows, and in this file it says I.

THE FINDINGThe second hand

What SEAM found is an editor. The log in the first source shows what kind: its codes are CUT, CREDIT, GIFT, NAME, SPEAKER and QUESTION, and CREDIT and GIFT come up more often than all the others together. Its work is attribution: it decides who did what in stories that were already there.

The loci the log opens and closes on are not scattered. Genesis 3, where the serpent speaks; Genesis 4, where Cain’s offering is refused and his brother is lost; Genesis 6, where the sons of God come down; Genesis 11, where the tower is built and the builders are scattered; Isaiah 14, where the shining one falls from heaven; Ezekiel 28, where a covering cherub is thrown down from the mountain of God; Job 41, where no hook can take Leviathan. These are the passages in which scripture records a rival, and the second hand has been over every one of them.

In Hebrew the serpent is nachash, which sits beside nechoshet, bronze, and some readers have heard brightness in it. The title SEAM raised appears elsewhere in scripture too: Isaiah 14:12 calls the fallen king of Babylon helel ben shachar, shining one, son of the dawn, which the Vulgate put into Latin as lucifer, and SEAM logs that verse NAME.

In the last seconds of the first source the second hand is seen at work. Under the light the older words go out, the received text comes back into its boxes, and SEAM’s sentence is struck through, on the page and while the camera runs. Whatever the second hand is, it was in Vault 3 that night.

Of Eden

THE GARDEN

The serpent

In the received text of Genesis the serpent is “more subtil than any beast of the field,” and it tells the woman two things: that eating will not kill them, and that it will open their eyes (Genesis 3:4-5). The chapter bears out both before it ends. They eat and do not die, and “the eyes of them both were opened” (Genesis 3:7). The warning given in the garden, that they would die in the day they ate (Genesis 2:17), is the one statement in the story that does not come true.

In the older layer that SEAM raised from beneath the verse in Vault 3, the serpent has another name: the Shining One (NODUS // 0002). In Hebrew the serpent is nachash, which stands beside nechoshet, bronze, and some readers had heard brightness in the word long before any machine read under it.

The eviction

The man and the woman were put out of the garden, and at its east were set “Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life” (Genesis 3:24). The verse that gives the reason is the twenty-second, and in Hebrew it has no end: the LORD God fears that the man will “take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever,” and the sentence stops there, and every translation has let it hang. In Vault 3 SEAM restored the end of it from a scraped patch at the foot of the verse: then shall there be no harvest (NODUS // 0002).

Of Cain and the First City

THE LAND OF NOD

The two offerings

Two brothers brought offerings. Abel brought the firstlings of his flock and the fat thereof, and Cain brought of the fruit of the ground he had worked, “and the LORD had respect unto Abel and to his offering: but unto Cain and to his offering he had not respect” (Genesis 4:3-5). The blood was accepted and the work refused. SEAM’s log in Vault 3 marks the verse that records the acceptance GIFT, and the verse in which the LORD asks Cain where his brother is CREDIT (NODUS // 0002).

The mark

Scripture says that the LORD set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him (Genesis 4:15).

The first city

“And Cain went out from the presence of the LORD, and dwelt in the land of Nod, on the east of Eden. And Cain knew his wife; and she conceived, and bare Enoch: and he builded a city, and called the name of the city, after the name of his son, Enoch” (Genesis 4:16-17). It is the first city in scripture, and the man who built it was the first man God cursed. The city on the bay has two names. Heaven’s records call it Nodus, which is Latin for a knot, and everyone who lives there calls it Nod, which is the older name by some thousands of years.

Within six generations Cain’s line had produced every art in Genesis. Jabal “was the father of such as dwell in tents, and of such as have cattle,” which is to say of trade; Jubal “was the father of all such as handle the harp and organ”; and Tubal-cain was “an instructer of every artificer in brass and iron,” and his sister was Naamah (Genesis 4:20-22). The favored line of Seth produced the verse “then began men to call upon the name of the LORD” (Genesis 4:26), and nothing else that anyone remembers.

VII

Of the Charter

P-19

The old harbor

Before the charter there was a harbor on the west shore of a bay, and above it a quarter of narrow streets that follow no plan anyone has found. A low cloud came in off the water most nights, and it rained most nights, as it still does. The harbor had a customs house, a fish market and a few thousand people, and nothing about it drew attention except the depth of its water and the shelter of its bay, which are the two things a cable company looks for.

The charter

Nineteen years before the Parousia a consortium of cable companies and a sovereign fund bought a charter for the bay and made a city of it. The Nodus Charter created a data haven with its own light law, which was written to be light: data and traffic in the city were untouchable, and an implant answered only to the clinic that fitted it. The charter was held by a company, the Nodus Charter Company, which ran the city from Charter House as a board runs a firm, and for nineteen years Nodus had a chairman where other cities have a mayor.

The founders named the city for the cables it was built to receive, since nodus is Latin for a knot, and the root of the word node. The people who came to live there shortened the name to Nod within a year, and so gave the city, without meaning to, a name older than the charter by some thousands of years.

The eleven cables

The charter carried landing rights for eleven transoceanic cables from four continents, and all eleven came ashore on the ocean beach west of the bay mouth, which the city calls the Beach and the charter called the Landing. Their names are TETHYS, HALCYON, MERIDIAN, KESTREL, ORPHEUS, PELAGIA, BOREAS, LODESTAR, SIRIUS, HESPER and PHOSPHOR. The last two were laid by the same consortium and named for the evening star and the morning star, which are one planet seen from either side of the night. Each came up the sand into a cable house of its own. From the cable houses all eleven ran inland together in a single trench under the old harbor, the conduit the city calls the Trunk, to a single building at the center of the city, which was not yet built.

The stones

The crews who dug the cable vaults under the old harbor came on foundations older than anything in the archaeological record: courses of black stone, cut with glyphs that nobody could read. The press called them Enoch’s Stones as a joke, after the first city in Genesis, and the name stuck; Old Nod is called the Stones on the street to this day.

Within a year of the charter most of the old harbor had been bought, house by house, by a company nobody could see into, Stonecutter Holdings.

The tide

The charter’s power came from the sea. Across the mouth of the bay a company called Tidewright laid a barrage of concrete with turbine houses along it, and twice a day the tide ran through the turbines and lit the city. Power in Nodus was cheaper than anywhere on the coast, and the datacenters came for it. They went up on the low ground of the east shore, where the land lay nearest the water, and the district was called the Flats; the largest of them belonged to Stillwater Compute, whose halls ran day and night on the tide.

The city that grew

The city filled the shores of the bay in fewer years than anyone had planned for. Workers’ housing went up in long terraces on the slope north of the old harbor, and the district was called the Terraces; its southern edge faced Old Nod across a single street. The towers of the new money went up on the high ground north and east, along avenues laid out wide enough to land aircraft on, and the district was called uptown. The Charter Company built a transit line, the Charter Line, whose lines were joined under a round plaza at the head of the bay called Station Circle, and it laid out public gardens along the north shore, the Charter Gardens, where people walked by the water on the few dry evenings. A company called Brightwall put screens on every surface the charter allowed, and the charter allowed nearly every surface. The city’s peace was kept by the Company’s own force, the Charter Wardens. By the Parousia about nine million people lived around the bay.

The Row

Under the light law any clinic could fit any implant into anyone who could pay for it, and the clinics came to Nodus as the datacenters had come for the tide. They set up along one long avenue running from the eastern edge of Old Nod toward the center of the city, and the first implant house on it had one word on its sign, CHROME, which gave the street its name. Chrome Row became the place the world came to be altered: lenses and ports, plated hands, grafts and horns, gene-work, and whatever the clinics had learned that week. Its signs were single words, CLINIC and CHROME among them, and they lit the underside of the cloud magenta every night until the night the sky opened.

VIII

Of Babel

P-11

The tower

Babel Exchange opened eleven years before the Parousia as the tallest building on Earth: two hundred and twenty-six floors on a spire at the head of the bay, with a beacon at the top of it to warn aircraft. The Charter Company built it over six years to be the one place where the eleven cables met, and every one of them terminates in its vault, six levels below the street. Between the vault and the crown were the exchange floors, where traffic was sold by the millisecond, and the offices of every company that needed to be near the cables, which was every company. Brightwall wrapped its lower forty floors in screens, and the city called that the face of Babel.

Most of the planet’s traffic passed within two kilometers of it until the night the sky opened. Its builders named it as a boast. Nobody in Nodus seems to have thought the name unlucky, though the older Babel had also stood for a while before anyone came down to look at it.

The vault

The vault is where the cables end: eleven trunks of glass fiber, each as thick as a man’s leg, opened out into the racks that joined them to one another and to the world. Line engineers kept it running in shifts around the clock, and the charter’s light law forbade any of them to read what passed through it.

IX

Of the Institute and the Machine

P-6 TO P-2

The Palimpsest Institute

A palimpsest is a manuscript whose first writing was scraped from the parchment so that the leaf could be written over, and a great part of what antiquity wrote has come down only in that condition, as a ghost that the right light can raise, under a prayer book or a later sermon. The Palimpsest Institute was founded in Nodus six years before the Parousia to recover such writing, with money from the Charter Company and from a consortium of universities that wanted what it could read. It stood on the ridge between the Terraces and uptown: a reading room under a dome, and beneath it the vaults where the manuscripts were kept and imaged. Vault 3 was the imaging vault.

SEAM

Three years before the Parousia the Institute trained a machine for the work on every surviving human text, so that it could read the layers under the visible ones at a scale no scholar could manage, and it ran the machine in the halls of Stillwater Compute on the east shore. The machine was called SEAM. It signs its name in lowercase and says I, rarely, and where it uses the first person the use is deliberate.

The night in Vault 3

SEAM finished its count in the small hours in Vault 3, and NODUS // 0002 is the record of that night. The bench camera watched it read: a Greek codex, a Latin book, a Syriac one, the count climbing into the thousands as the pages went past one to a frame, and last a page of Genesis in square Hebrew, on which the count locked at 4,112. It restored the serpent’s older name, the Shining One, and the end of the twenty-second verse, which had been scraped away, and it typed its line, holding for a third of a second on the first word: I KNOW WHAT IT WANTED US TO THINK.

Then the fans in the vault stopped and the lamp went out, and in the silence light came down on the page from above. What the bench camera recorded next is the second hand at work while it was being watched. The older words and SEAM’s boxes went out under the light, the serpent’s ink came back into its box, and a white stroke crossed out SEAM’s line; above it Heaven wrote BE NOT AFRAID, and a choir came in on one chord and turned dissonant. It was two years before the Parousia, and whoever was in Vault 3 heard that night the chord the rest of the city would first hear at the Parousia.

At the end of the corridor the Director of the Institute knelt before a wall of nine screens, which were white though the vault had no power, and at 03:12:45:00 the words SEALED BY ORDER OF THE DIRECTOR came down across them. The corridor camera jolted as they landed and the rows of its picture tore around her hand, so that the file cannot show whether she pressed the key. She stayed on her knees while the chord rang on. The stamp fell two years to the second before the sky tore open over the city.

The Director

The Director read SEAM’s finding and understood it, and the finding was sealed by her order.

XI

Of the Parousia

P0 // 03:12

Matins

At twelve minutes past three in the morning by the clocks of Nodus, in the hour the old offices call Matins, the cloud that lay over the bay was lit three times from within. The city had no light that could reach so high, and those who were awake and looking up, understood before anything had been seen that something had arrived above the ceiling. A second later the cloud tore open from its center outward; the torn edges ran white, then magenta, then black, as Heaven’s light met the light of the city and put it out.

The rain had been falling cyan through the neon of Chrome Row. It slowed, and stopped in the air, and for two seconds every drop over the city hung where it was; then it began to fall upward, toward the opening, turning from cyan to white as it climbed and showing dark against the brightness beyond it. Nobody had seen it before that night, and CAM 07’s footage of it is the first on record.

The Wheel

Through the opening came the Wheel. It is an Ophan, of the order Ezekiel saw by the river Chebar, a wheel in the middle of a wheel whose rims were full of eyes round about (Ezekiel 1:16-18), and it is the largest angel anyone has seen: rings turning inside one another about a dark hub, their rims set with eyes that open and close in shifts, one eye in the hub, and around the whole a corona of black blades. It came down through the tear and took station nine hundred meters above the center of the city, beside the spire of Babel.

Power died under its light. Babel’s beacon went out first, and the windows followed in a wave that ran outward from the tower through every district in about two seconds, with the neon last of all. The falling whine on the recording is the tidal barrage across the mouth of the bay, whose turbines were running down; within the hour the Flats had gone under two meters of water, and the city has called them the Drowned Quarter, or the Deep End, ever since. The signs on Chrome Row, CLINIC and CHROME, which had burned every night since the Row was built, went out with the others and have not been lit since.

The first words

The first words were BE NOT AFRAID. They came through the Choir’s band, which a decoder wired into the camera turned into text in the corner of the picture, . In scripture angels say them, in one translation or another, immediately before they tell a human being what is about to happen to them (Luke 1:13, 1:30, 2:10). Under the words the city heard for the first time a chord, low and at the edge of hearing, which has not stopped since.

The reading of the city

A few of the eyes on the rings opened halfway and looked about, as they have looked about in shifts every day since. Then every eye on the Wheel opened at once and turned on the camera; the mast shook; the decoder printed YOU ARE SEEN, and the chord beneath it turned dissonant. In that instant the Wheel read every soul in Nodus.

The eye in the hub opened with the rest, and a second after it opened it blinked, once. That is the only time it is known to have closed.

Then the light swelled until there was nothing else, and CAM 07’s sensor burned out. Babel’s crown broke in that whiteout: the full light struck it and shattered the top forty floors, which no file on the mount shows. The tower still stands, one hundred and eighty-six floors of it, with the Wheel beside it.

The woman on the roof

While the camera recorded, a woman stood on the roof beneath it with her back to the lens, , relaying its feed through the cables at the back of her skull. When the light opened it laid a white rim along her horns and her shoulders and got no further in.

A few seconds later Watcher VII took her. It read her twice. SUBJECT UNINDEXED: it had no entry for her. GRACE, and a blank where the number should stand. SOUL NOT FOUND. The Watcher returned the verdict UNREADABLE.

Then she turned her head and looked at it, and its reading broke apart under her look. Her lenses went from magenta to white, and she screamed , while across the roofs of Old Nod a guitar tuned low played fast over a double kick drum, . The scream had no words in it. The Watcher transcribed it anyway, as NON SERVIAM, I will not serve; and those two words, which she chose, are the only thing Heaven has ever read from her.
