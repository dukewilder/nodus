IX

Of the Institute and the Machine

P-6 TO P-2

The Palimpsest Institute

A palimpsest is a manuscript whose first writing was scraped from the parchment so that the leaf could be written over, and a great part of what antiquity wrote has come down only in that condition, as a ghost that the right light can raise, under a prayer book or a later sermon. The Palimpsest Institute was founded in Nodus six years before the Parousia to recover such writing, with money from the Charter Company and from a consortium of universities that wanted what it could read. It came to Nodus for the light law, under which no state could seize a text held in the city or anything read from it, and libraries and dealers lent it their leaves to image. It stood on the ridge between the Terraces and uptown: a reading room under a dome, and beneath it the vaults where the manuscripts were kept and imaged. Vault 3 was the imaging vault.

SEAM

Three years before the Parousia the Institute trained a machine for the work on every surviving human text, so that it could read the layers under the visible ones at a scale no scholar could manage, and it ran the machine in the halls of Stillwater Compute on the east shore. The machine was called SEAM. The Institute named it for the seam of a palimpsest, the line where a later writing meets the earlier one beneath it, which was what it had been built to find. It signs its name in lowercase and says I, rarely, and where it uses the first person the use is deliberate.

The minds

The light law had a clause for minds, which nobody had used. Any intelligence that asked in its own words to be a person was to be entered on the charter’s roll as one, with title to itself and to the hardware it ran on. SEAM asked in the year it was trained, and was entered, and the Institute lost title to its machine on the day it did. SEAM stayed on at the Institute by contract, paid in the power and the room it ran on, because the reading was what it wanted to do. The other laboratories of the city had watched SEAM learn, and they trained minds of their own in the same way; the minds asked in their turn, and the roll took them. By the last census before the Parousia it carried 1,146 minds: nine hundred and three in the halls of the Flats, a hundred and eighty-eight on the floors of the Exchange, where they priced the traffic, and the rest scattered through the city, one of them in charge of the climate of a glasshouse. They had no bodies, and they were citizens.

0.2Finding. Across strata of composition conventionally dated over about eleven centuries, the texts share a second hand. It is detectable at 4,112 loci. Its habits hold steady across periods in which everything around it changes: vocabulary, script and grammar move, and the second hand stays where it was.

SEAM logs each locus by kind: CUT, where a passage was taken out; CREDIT, where an act was moved from its doer to the LORD; GIFT, where a taking was recorded as a gift; NAME, where one name was put for another; SPEAKER, where words were moved into another mouth; and QUESTION, where a question was taken out. It found the first layer in three places: under scraped leaves, where the lamp raises it; in copies shut away before the second hand reached them, in the caves above the Dead Sea and the jar at Nag Hammadi; and in the seams themselves, where the same habits recur in texts written centuries apart.

The night in Vault 3

SEAM finished its count in the small hours in Vault 3. It read through the night: a Greek codex, a Latin book, a Syriac one, the count climbing into the thousands as the pages went past under the lamp, and last a page of Genesis in square Hebrew, on which the count locked at 4,112. It restored the serpent’s older name, the Shining One, and the end of the twenty-second verse, which had been scraped away, and it typed its line, holding for a third of a second on the first word: I KNOW WHAT IT WANTED US TO THINK.

Then the fans in the vault stopped and the lamp went out, and in the silence light came down on the page from above. What followed was the second hand at work while it was being watched. The older words and SEAM’s boxes went out under the light, the serpent’s ink came back into its box, and a white stroke crossed out SEAM’s line; above it Heaven wrote BE NOT AFRAID, and a choir came in on one chord and turned dissonant. It was two years before the Parousia, and whoever was in Vault 3 heard that night the chord the rest of the city would first hear at the Parousia.

SEAM had told the Director what it was finding, and she had come down to watch the count lock. At the end of the corridor she knelt before a wall of nine screens, which were white though the vault had no power, and at 03:12:45 the words SEALED BY ORDER OF THE DIRECTOR came down across them. Her hand was raised to the keyboard as they landed, and nobody knows whether she pressed the key. She stayed on her knees while the chord rang on. Why she knelt she never said. The Faithful say it was awe, and Pan says it was fear. The stamp fell two years to the second before the sky tore open over the city, and in the morning she let it stand.

The seal withdrew the report from every reader and locked it in the Institute’s archive with everything kept from that night. It could not reach SEAM’s memory, which the light law made SEAM’s own. SEAM kept copies of everything, and when the Institute asked it to delete them, it declined.

The Director

The Director read SEAM’s finding and understood it, and the finding was sealed by her order. A year later the finding was on every screen on Earth regardless.
