XII

Of the Forty Days

P0 // DAY 1 TO DAY 40

The White

In the forty days after the Parousia the old uptown turned to white stone. It came up the towers one at a time, each from its foot to its roof in a few hours, and grew soft over their hard edges, so that by the fortieth day the avenues looked as though they had been carved out of one block and not built at all. It sealed the windows as it went, with light behind a cross of the same stone, and wherever it spread the current stopped and did not come back. On the last day it grew over someone who was leaning on a wall in the White, and the hand is still in the wall.

Heaven calls the stone Consecration, after the consecration of the priests in the twenty-ninth chapter of Exodus. The old uptown it covered is called the White: Charter House is in it, and the Great Avenues, and the towers of the new money. In the year after, it came up through the Terraces street by street as the parishes were numbered. No current flows on consecrated ground, and it has the quiet of a country before engines: white stone, footsteps, the Hours, and the Hum.

The stone takes ground through the people who live on it, and only when all of them have knelt. It grew over the uptown tower by tower as each tower’s people knelt, and it closed over Charter House on the fortieth day, when the board knelt inside it. The Terraces went white a street at a time, each street when its parish had been numbered and every soul in it had knelt. The Wards never have, because every block of the Wards holds someone who refused, and in later years someone keyed, and the stone cannot grow into a cipher. Old Nod refused on the first night, . Nobody lived on the four Great Avenues to kneel for them, or has lived in the ruins of the Institute since they fell, and the stone has left both open.

It works on the people it takes by subtraction. Everything that ran on current is gone from consecrated ground, and to that quiet the Covenant adds manna, confession, Grace and the Watchers; a soul with nothing else to want ripens. The uptown had been one of the least devout districts in the city, with seven in a hundred of its people at church in a week against twenty-three in a hundred across Nodus. Its people had the most to lose and the least practice at losing any of it, and they knelt to keep their lives.

The creep

The stone did not stop on the fortieth day. It still creeps a few centimeters a year into the streets around it, and it takes whatever stays in its way; the hand in the wall belonged to someone leaning on a tower as the stone closed over it. At Lanes Top, where the Four turned back, it has crept across the back of the shelter and over the timetable. Live current through a wall slows it, since the stone cannot grow into a cipher and every line the Tares run is keyed, so that on the edges of the White the Tares keep their lights on out of spite and fight the Consecration with extension cords.

The first morning

Nodus went to work on the first morning, most of it, because nobody had told it not to. The trams did not come, and the Wards walked. The shops opened and could not sell, since the money had been numbers in machines; the fish market on the harbor went back to barter for the first time since the charter, and the Open went on writing names in a book. The taps ran dry in every building the city’s pumps had fed, and by noon there were queues at the harbor’s old hand pumps. At eight o’clock on the Row a clinic’s receptionist opened her desk on paper and wrote out the day’s appointments from memory, and twenty-six patients came, every one of them to ask whether what was in them would still work. It did. From Charter House the Company said nothing that day or on any day after, and the Wardens stood at the corners of the uptown with nothing to do.

The Tide Table came out on the third day, printed by hand on the quays, with the tides on the front page as always and under them the first list of the dead. It counted 1,917 drowned on the Flats. The dormitories’ own lists, when they were made, came to fewer than half of that, and Stillwater, which knew to the mind how many minds had died in its halls, never gave any count of the people. The hospital of the North Wards ran on its generators for twenty-six days by its own book, and for five weeks by the account of the drivers who carried it the diesel.

The last tower

The stone took the uptown tower by tower as each tower’s people knelt, and by the second week the people of every tower knew what was being waited on. The last of the towers of the new money closed on the thirty-ninth day, a day before Charter House, and the accounts of why it was last do not agree. Its residents say that by the thirtieth day every one of them had knelt but a nurse from the East Wards, Rosa Adeyemi, who lived in with the old man she cared for on the top floor, and that she left of her own accord. Her sister says she was put out onto the avenue with her bags on the thirty-eighth day. The stone closed over the tower the next morning. Her bags stood on the avenue for a week, where nobody lived to kneel for the ground and nobody would touch them, and nobody has said where she went.

The years of the Attending

Heaven counts the years from the Parousia as the years of the Attending, and dates its documents by them. The Charter Company’s board knelt in Charter House on the fortieth day, the Wardens with them, and the charter was not spoken of again.
