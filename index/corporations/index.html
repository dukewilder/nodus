The company that held the charter and ran Nodus as a board runs a firm. It has been gone since the fortieth day after the Parousia. A consortium of cable companies and a sovereign fund formed it to hold the charter, a coastal state leased it the bay and the land round it for ninety-nine years and gave up its law there, and for nineteen years the city had a chairman where other cities have a mayor.

It lived on rent. The carriers paid it to land their cables, the companies that came for the light law paid it for their floors, and the Wards paid it by the month for flats on grids of its own, numbered. It built Babel Exchange and took a share of every trade there, and it built the Charter Line, the airfield, the Acre, the Company’s schools, High Meadow, the Charter Gardens on the north shore, which are the Salt Gardens now, and the sign on a roof in the Stones that told the cable ships the city’s name. It kept the peace with its own force, the Charter Wardens, kept the charter’s roll at the Registry on the Great Avenues, and in nineteen years held no election, since the charter asked for none.

The city saw the Company give way once, in P-8, when the Harbor and Freight Union stopped the port for twenty-three days over the night shift and the Company settled.

Charter House turned white in the forty days after the Parousia. The board knelt inside it on the fortieth day, and the charter was not spoken of again. The Company had said nothing from the first morning on. Its lease runs to P+80, sixty-six years past the present, and nobody has come for the rent. Most of the companies it brought to the bay have outlived it.

THE CHARTER WHOLE, IN TWELVE ARTICLES

Under the charter the Company kept a house style of glass, brushed steel and numbers, every flat on its grids coded over the door, every Warden in charter black, every board meeting minuted to the second. On the fortieth day the style turned white without a line of it moving, and Charter House keeps the old numbering and the squared corridors where the board went down on its knees at the long table.

The exchange in the tallest building on Earth, which has never closed. Every cable that comes ashore at the Landing ends in that vault, six levels under the tower, and the undernet that joins Pan to the undercities of the world runs through it. Since P+9 Babel Exchange has traded on the floors the Tares hold, with the Babel Line in the middle floors over its head.

The Charter Company built the tower to two hundred and twenty-six floors as a boast, named it for one, and opened it in P-11. Traffic was sold on its floors by the millisecond, priced by a hundred and eighty-eight minds on the charter’s roll, and most of the planet’s passed within two kilometers of it. A trading house that wanted its orders in ahead of every other on Earth took room near the vault, the glass towers round the spire filled with every company that needed to be near the cables, and Brightwall’s screens wrapped the lower forty floors as the face of Babel.

At the Parousia its beacon was the first light in the city to die. At 03:12:51 the light broke the crown, and the top forty floors came down on the plaza with the night shift at work in them. The roster died with the tower’s machines, and the minds that priced the traffic died with its power. What was left of the night shift kept the floor open by candle on the first morning, with nothing to sell, and the traders who went dark have sold traffic on the undernet ever since, for charged batteries at first and for silver since the Concord.

Its floor keeps one board for the Seraphim burning on the floors above it, headed THE NEIGHBORS, with the week’s floors lost and taken, and its traders cheer a floor the Babel Line takes back the way they cheer a rate going up.

Babel’s traders keep the old floor’s habits by candle and keyed lamp, sharp suits gone ragged, ports open at the nape, lenses flickering with the rate and voices hoarse from shouting it. The floor nearest the vault smells of sweat, solder and the burns brought down the stairs, and a trader who closes a fat sale climbs toward the Line, bares the chest and has the figure tattooed there in Babel Line soot.

The house on the ridge that recovered erased writing, and the house SEAM came from, in ruins since P+1. Its ruins are called the Scriptorium. Pilgrims leave keys on its steps. Below them, at COPIES, the keyed of the ridge copy the Restored Genesis by hand.

It was founded in P-6 to recover erased manuscripts, the texts scraped off parchment and written over, with money from the Charter Company and from a consortium of universities that wanted what it could read. It kept a reading room under a dome on the ridge, with vaults beneath it, and it chose Nodus for the light law, under which no state could seize a text held in the city, so libraries and dealers lent it their leaves to image.

Its staff was small and its work patient. Imagers lit a leaf at many wavelengths under the lamp, conservators kept the leaves, and scholars read what came up, most of them lent by the universities for a term. In P-3 it grew SEAM, the first mind anyone grew, and trained it to read beneath the writing of every surviving human text, and SEAM asked in its own words to be a person and became the first mind on the charter’s roll. SEAM ran in Stillwater’s halls on the Flats by a contract the Institute paid, and stayed on by contract once it was a person. In P-2 what SEAM found was sealed by order of the Institute’s Director.

In P+1 its stacks went onto the fire at the Burn, and the Faithful pulled the Institute down around its reading room.

The parishes call its ruins the serpent’s library, and the Deacons of the Terraces preach that the fire of the first day of P+1 was the LORD’s own editing.

The Institute lived in quiet and lamplight, conservators in gloves and masks bent over scraped leaves under the dome, and the ruin on the ridge keeps the colors of that work, burned vellum, soot and the violet of the imaging lamps still on the reading room’s broken walls. Pilgrims climb to the Scriptorium with a word of the Restored Genesis tattooed on the tongue.

The firm whose current lights Pan, Old Nod, the Row and the Deep End, as its barrage once lit the whole city. Its crews rebuilt the turbines off the broken end of the barrage as the tidal taps of the Drowned Quarter, keyed for the Tares, and Tidewright sells every tide’s current to the Exchange for silver, on the flood and on the ebb, and the Exchange sells it on to Pan as charge.

An old firm of sea walls and tide mills, founded in P-146, it had held back the sea from other people’s coasts for more than a century when the Company brought it to Nodus in the charter’s first year to lay the barrage across the mouth of the bay. It closed the barrage in P-18, and twice a day after that the tide ran through its turbine houses and lit the city. The Company bought the current, and the cheap power brought Stillwater and the rest of the datacenters to the east shore. Its crews lived on Turbine Row in the East Wards and sent their children to the Company’s school there.

The barrage was a dam with a lock at its western end for the harbor’s boats and a row of turbine houses along its top, and machines on the grid held its gates against the tide. The turbines ran down at 03:12:46 on the night of the Parousia. The gates let go a little before high water and the sea tore out a span of the dam. The pumps that kept the Flats dry had died with the grid, the Flats’ own dykes broke within the hour, and the Flats, reclaimed below low water, were under the sea. Nobody has closed the Breach. In the second year the crews went back out to the broken end to build the taps, and part of the city runs on Tidewright’s current again.

Its engineers like to say that the LORD boasts in Jeremiah of the sand He set “for the bound of the sea by a perpetual decree, that it cannot pass it” (Jeremiah 5:22), that the sea came through the barrage on the night of the Parousia anyway, and that Tidewright has sold it back to Pan by the tide, through the Exchange, ever since.

Tidewright’s crews are bodies built for the sea wall, barrel-chested and salt-scarred, gilled for the dives on the taps, with steel shins for the weed and the rocks, and each wears the tide table of the year they joined tattooed round the wrist. Turbine Row hangs the barrage’s broken gates in its street as sculpture, and the crews repaint them every year with the Throne’s filthiest name and the minute the sea came in.

Stillwater keeps its halls in Pan corporate and cold, racks in ruled rows and staff in grey with the company’s name on the back, and the Tares call it the nearest thing in the dark to a parish. Its engineers break the look with their bodies, ports and heat-sink fins grafted down the spine, and at 03:12 on the anniversary they row out in black to set a lamp on the water over Hall Nine.

Brightwall

The screen company that lights the walls of Pan. Its screens hang along the old platforms, over the Night Market and down the tunnels of the Charter Line, sold by the second to anyone with something to sell, and each has its camera across the way again, proving it second by second. Above the street its screens have been dark since the Parousia, the face of Babel among them, dark glass wherever the white stone has not grown over them.

Founded in P-67, it had lit the streets of other cities for half a century before it came to Nodus in P-15, where it covered every surface the charter allowed, and the charter allowed nearly every surface. It wrapped the lower forty floors of Babel in screens, which the city called the face of Babel, and it kept its city office in a glass tower across the plaza, where it could watch the face from its own roof.

It sold the city’s surfaces by the second, and it proved every second. Each screen it owned had a proof camera on a mast across the street, which matched the screen against what Brightwall was paid to show, and each of its 1,204 sites on Earth matched its own screens the same way. A screen that went wrong was taken back from its site by an override.

For eleven days in the year before the Parousia every one of its screens showed the Restored Genesis, and nothing Brightwall did would take it down. On the first night all 1,204 of its sites on Earth failed in about a second, and it tried five times to take back the face of Babel. Its screens died with the grid at 03:12:46. Its first screen in Pan went up in the year after the Concord, at the foot of the stairs from the Burn.

The most-bought second in Pan, every night at Compline, is a frame of the Wheel’s eye with a price tag hung across it, paid for by a different coven each night and booked a year ahead.

Brightwall’s screens show Pan to itself at its loudest, a coven’s horns four meters high, a Spare’s new chrome turning in close-up, a naked dancer of the Lantern Houses slowed to a crawl, every frame proved by the camera across the way. The filthiest seconds sell best, and a screen bought to run a parish hymn has its glass in the gutter before the second verse.

The night watch of every cable house was at its post when the grid died on the night of the Parousia, and stayed at it. At the Landing a light moves under the water along the cable trenches. It is Leviathan’s.

The eldest carrier keeps the first message ever sent along a wire on a brass plate in its cable house, WHAT HATH GOD WROUGHT (Numbers 23:23), and the night watch chalks a new answer under it every week.

The cable houses keep a sailor’s dress gone strange, night watches in oilskin and brass buttons with keyed ports at the temple and lenses that track the light under the water, and each crew tattoos its cable’s name across the back in letters a hand high. Chrome Row under the street is the city’s catwalk, every surgeon wearing their own best work, one with four hands, a nurse lit with lantern skin to the eyebrows, a clerk with a ledger’s columns raised in ridges across the scalp. The Tares painted its old slogan back across the dark green tower in letters four floors high, WHAT HE KEPT FROM YOU, and the Wheel has looked at it every day since.

Twelvefruit’s clinics are the most beautiful rooms in Pan, green glass salvaged from the dark tower and keyed lamps the color of ripe fruit, and the course is taken lying naked in warm gel while a gene-worker paints the injection site in gold. Its customers keep their best year and dress it like an altar to themselves, and the Golden Calf can pick a Twelvefruit body across the bar by its shine.
