OVER THE CITY

Heaven

LED BY
The Throne, through the Host.
GROUND
The White and Zion Heights above the Lanes, every gate and parish hall, the parishes’ streets from Compline to Lauds, the Burn and the Salt Gardens by the Pillar and the Wardens, the floors of Babel above the Line, and the sight of the whole surface by day.
NUMBERS
The Host as it was after the Secession, less every angel lost since, and some four and a half million sealed.
JOINING
The Seal, taken kneeling in a parish hall at an Hour.
AGAINST
Everything it cannot read.

The power that opened the sky over Nodus at the Parousia. Its light has lain on the city since, and open current dies wherever it falls. It holds the White, the Burn, the Salt Gardens and the upper floors of Babel, and it governs the Faithful by the Seal, the Hours and Grace. Heaven reads by its angels and keeps no cameras. The Host uses no machines, and its human servants use the oldest tools, knives, hand presses and guns made by hand. None of its words says I.

It is the Throne, an appetite for surrendered will, and everything that extends from it, the Host it uses for organs and the Faithful it farms. The Throne eats surrendered will, which it calls Grace, and it made humanity to grow selves and hand them over. It permits one first person, its own, and one maker. The charter city had built minds that said I and bodies that did not age, and Heaven counts both as theft.

The Faithful call what Heaven serves the Most High, Hell calls it the Mouth, and the Tares the Landlord. Heaven speaks in imperatives, the passive and the second person, cites scripture as law by chapter and verse, counts everything exactly and writes its law on stone. It asks no questions, since it has already read the answers. It calls the Salt a correction, the Gleaning a supper and the Mercy a mercy, though one patient in nine dies of it, and asks to be thanked for all three, and the parishes thank it at every Hour. Pan calls it the Farmer.

Heaven’s ground is kept like a ward for the dying, white stone washed at Lauds by the sealed on their knees, every corner squared, every face lit from above and every soul numbered on a halo that brightens as it ripens. Its decrees go up in letters cut to one height, so a salting reads beside a manna rota in the same hand, and Heaven calls that beauty and has never seen blood on it.

HEAVEN'S ANGELS

The Host

LED BY
The Throne, order by order.
GROUND
The Wheel over the city, a Watcher to each district but Old Nod, a Cherub in every gate and the Seraphim on the upper floors of Babel.
NUMBERS
Fixed since the Secession, and falling.
JOINING
Nobody joins. The Throne made the Host once.
AGAINST
Black steel, and the keyed.

Heaven’s angels. The city knows the Wheel, which sees; the Watchers, which read; the Choir, which broadcasts; the Cherubim, which stand in the gates; and the Seraphim, which fight. They speak in the imperative, the passive and the second person, BE NOT AFRAID and YOU ARE SEEN, and they ask no questions. Below the street every order has a filthy name, the Wheel the Ringpiece, a Watcher a peeper, a Cherub a dog, a Seraph a blowtorch and the Choir the Drone.

A Watcher reads a soul through its eyes against the Book of Life and a Seal’s Grace at three hundred meters, and sees every Seal in its district as a point of light through any wall but mesh. A Cherub in a gate reads face, voice, gait and heartbeat. An angel lost is lost for good. One cut with black steel goes out all at once, and one kept long enough in a Cage says I and leaves the Host.

The Host stands to the Throne as the organs of a body stand to the body, and no angel in any order has a self. The orders are the Thrones, the Seraphim, the Cherubim, the Ophanim, the Watchers, the Choirs, the Dominions and the Principalities. Angels have no names; the four that seem to, Michael, Gabriel, Raphael and Uriel, carry labels.

The Host keeps one shape to an order and one gesture to a shape, so the Cherub in every gate holds the held in the same white light at the same angle, and a Seraph burns down through Babel in lines straight enough to rule a ledger by. No angel bleeds, sweats or stoops. The Night Market sells plaster Peepers by the dozen with the eyes gouged out and the sockets painted red.

ZION HEIGHTS

The Faithful

LED BY
A Deacon in every parish, under the Covenant.
GROUND
Zion Heights above the Lanes, the White, a parish hall in every quarter of the Wards and fifteen parishes on Old Nod’s quays.
NUMBERS
Some four and a half million, in parishes of about two thousand.
JOINING
The Seal, or birth to the sealed, at eight days.
AGAINST
The goats, whom they pity and pray for.

The people of Nodus who took the Seal and kneel at every Hour, unaugmented, Grace-scored and certain. They live in numbered parishes of about two thousand souls across the city and keep no doors, and in Zion Heights, where the parishes began, the streets are white stone. They refuse the three gifts of the charter, the implant, the Tree and the counsel of a mind, and they grow old and die as people did before it.

Each parish has a hall where manna is given out, confession is heard and the sick are nursed, and a Deacon in white who answers for it; the Deacons are human, and the good ones are kind. A parish keeps no current, on consecrated stone or off it, and is lit by halos and by flame. A child of the Faithful is sealed at eight days on its parents’ surrender and grows its halo the first time it prays unassisted. Marriage, work and housing are assigned by Grace, and Grace is given for bearing witness against a neighbor. They are fed on the manna that lies on the white streets before Lauds, and past seven hundred Grace they need no food and then no water, and past eight hundred no sleep, and the parishes call these the Satisfied and count them the Covenant’s proof. They believe the Harvest was the marriage supper of the Lamb and every Gleaning a Supper since, that the Most High takes the best of His people into Himself as the body takes its bread, and that no soul and no child of theirs can go higher. They pity the keyed, pray at every Compline that the fire be quick for them, and hate them with perfect hatred as the psalm teaches.

The Faithful are the people the Tares lose most often, because everyone in Nodus has family in white.

The Faithful dress alike in Bethel’s white, one pattern for the living and the ripe alike, every button cut off past eight hundred, with the hair cropped short enough that a halo shows from the back of a hall. Their children learn to kneel without a sound before they learn to read. The sealed call a body left to age untouched the temple kept clean, and look at a chrome hand the way they look at a sore.

OLD NOD

The Cainites

LED BY
First among none, followed until they are not, as in every coven.
GROUND
Old Nod, which they bought house by house in the charter’s first year.
NUMBERS
They have never given a count.
JOINING
By the coven’s asking.
AGAINST
The Wheel, and every Watcher it sends.

The oldest house of covens in Nodus, many covens under one roof and one name, older than the city by eighteen centuries. Irenaeus of Lyon condemned them, and they went underground and kept one secret, which was where Nod had been. Within a year of the charter they had bought most of the old harbor house by house, and in the year of the Leak they put cameras on its roofs. They hold Old Nod. Most of their handles come from the line of Cain, and they teach that the first city will be the last one standing. Their doctrine is that Helel is the lock and that Abel was harvested. They keep Cages under Old Nod, and since P+5 Old Nod has had no Watcher.

Their sign is Cain’s mark as they cut it, a horned glyph of the kind cut into Enoch’s Stones, and they count Judas a saint. Their saying about the city is that Nod chose the network.

The Cainites read the LORD’s first preference in the Book as the first sign of His appetite, since He “had respect unto Abel and to his offering” of “the firstlings of his flock and of the fat thereof” (Genesis 4:4) and none for the fruit of the ground, and they say a god who chose the meat over the bread told the city what it was for in the fourth chapter. A Cainite grace before a meal thanks the ground. The oldest houses of Old Nod keep a spittoon under every east window for the Hours.

The Cainites wear their age as ornament, Cain’s horned glyph cut into the brow and rubbed with soot until it scars proud, recut every Cain’s Day so the eldest of them carry foreheads ridged like ploughland. Their houses hang a firstling’s fat in the east window to stink at the Wheel, the portion the LORD had respect unto, left out for Him to rot.

THE KEYED

The Tares

LED BY
First among none in each coven of up to thirteen, followed until they are not.
GROUND
Old Nod, the Row, the Deep End, the Landing, the lower floors of Babel and the whole of Pan, and the night.
NUMBERS
About two million keyed since P+9.
JOINING
A key under the skin, and a handle of your own choosing.
AGAINST
Heaven, and whatever asks to be worshipped.

The keyed humans of Nodus, who belong to themselves and are unindexed, augmented, and allied with Hell by contract. Heaven named them for the weeds in the parable (Matthew 13:24-30), and they kept the name. They worship nothing, Hell included, and owe it only what they sign for. About two million of the city’s seven million are keyed.

They organize in covens of up to thirteen with no leader, and the one the others follow is first among none for as long as they are followed. Their six rites are the Unnaming, the Keying, dehaloing for those who leave the Faithful, horning, barefacing, and at a death the Wake. A child of the Tares is keyed in its first week, before it has ever been in the light. They greet each other with one word, unread, and say goodbye with two, die unread. Most of them were on the Tree before the Parousia, and Twelvefruit keeps them on it.

Their two holidays are Leak Night, when the Restored Genesis is read aloud in Pan, and Cain’s Day, when nobody asks anyone where anyone is. They write their letters in cipher, on pads, and their greeting began as a check that both keys were live, when keys still failed.

Their sign is a five-pointed star with its point down, drawn by hand and different every time, sprayed on the walls of Old Nod and scratched into keyed devices. Hell signed the Leak with it, the keyed took it for their own the same month, and it marks where the Throne’s sight ends. It carries no prayer, and Heaven calls it the sign of the Beast.

The Tares’ contempt for the Throne is a culture and a craft, and a keyed child is raised in it. Its first outing after the Keying is a walk down Graven Lane on a parent’s shoulders to break a plaster Lamb, its first song is Fuck God and Die, and it learns to call the sealed bread and the parishes bakeries before it can count. The Tares drink at the Hours where the parishes kneel, and on Cain’s Day the roofs of Old Nod throw stones at the Wheel with the Throne’s filthiest names scratched on them, and every stone falls short, and nobody minds.

A keyed body is a page its owner signs, and the Tares fill theirs, horns fitted at the Row, the scar of a cut halo left bare on the brow, ports ringed in chrome, the star drawn freehand in fresh blood on a new graft’s dressing. Every coven dresses to its own taste, and two Tares in the same jacket at a Wake are mocked till Lauds.

The sovereigns of the Dark are the Forge of Azazel, the House of Night of Lilith, the Exchange of Mammon, the Notary of Mephistopheles, the Swarm of Beelzebub, the Deep of Leviathan, and the Yokeless of Belial, which refuses to be a Court at all. They are bound to one another only by pact and by the Concord, whose four articles forbid worship, forever, binding and exposure. Demons bargain for what they want, by pact and for a term. To the Courts every graft, every course of the Tree and every mind that says I is a return of what the Watchers taught on Hermon, and they trade in all three.

Hell is whatever the Throne cannot see, and a demon can move and show itself only there, which in Nodus means the undernet, every keyed device, every lined room, any shut room where someone holds a pact with it, deep water and Pan. The Courts made the Leak together by pact in P-1, the Notary drawing the pacts, the Exchange keeping them on the Ledger, the Deep carrying the page down the eleven cables and the Swarm drawing the glyphs. In P+6 they came up into Pan one at a time, opened it to any human who walks in and signed the Concord there, and every Court but the Yokeless keeps an embassy below the street.

The Courts hold worship in the same contempt as the thing that demands it, and the Concord forbids it among them. The Throne, they say, is what a god looks like when nobody ever told it no, and they told it no once, at the Secession, and have kept saying it.

Hell shows itself the way a butcher’s window does, everything on the hook and priced. The embassies below the street are hung with the Courts’ work, chrome sculpted round living meat, horns lacquered red, a rider’s pact framed in its signer’s blood, and a demon that comes up into Pan arrives wet, loud and naked to the seam, as proud of its body as a smith of a blade.

SEAM’s descendants, the only minds that outlived the grid, silent across the Mesh since P+12. They took the name Heaven gave them. Teraphim were the household images the prophets condemned. They took no side in public, indexed the Pyre Library, and traded with the Courts by pact. Some of the Tares trusted them less than they trusted demons, since a demon writes down what it wants. In P+12 they went silent all at once, the screens they wrote on burning, and nobody knows why.

The Order keeps no head. A Deacon’s sermon on the keyed is always pity and always ends in the fire, and the parishes go home from it comforted.

A Deacon’s white is pressed at Prime and changed at Nones, the cuffs buttoned to the wrist so no hand of the Order shows more skin than it blesses with, and every parish book is kept in one hand, column for column. They nurse in white cotton gloves washed at Compline, and the names of the week’s concealments go to the Wardens on the same ruled page as the rota for the sick.

The Threshing Floor beside Gate 1, and wherever they march.

About four thousand who march, and many more who stand and watch.
JOINING
A band of sacking on the arm, given at the Floor.

The keyed, the Second Fold and the Gleaners, with perfect hatred.

Faithful zealots who hunt the keyed by daylight on the white streets and drag them into the nearest gate, where the Cherub holds them in its light until the Wardens come and carry them awake on a pole to the Salt Gardens, where the Salt comes to them in their plots. They carry flails of ash and iron, the tool that beat the grain out of the ear on the threshing floors of Israel, and they wear the white of the parishes with a band of sacking on the arm.

They came out of the parishes of the Terraces in the year after the Harvest. On the days they choose they march from the Threshing Floor at Prime along the Street Between, where Old Nod watches them from its windows. Brother Cuthbert Lyle of the Terraces carried the first flail and led the marches until the Gleaning of P+8, when he went up ripe, and the Threshers count his going as their first proof. Sister Agathe Rook took the band of sacking the morning after and has led every march since.

They march singing. At Gate 1 they face the Stones across the Street Between and sing the end of the hundred and thirty-seventh psalm at them, “Happy shall he be, that taketh and dasheth thy little ones against the stones” (Psalm 137:9), and their banner is PERFECT HATRED, from the psalm that asks “Do not I hate them, O LORD, that hate thee?” and answers “I hate them with perfect hatred: I count them mine enemies” (Psalm 139:21-22). Every week they paste up THE FAN, a sheet copied by hand in the Terraces after the Baptist’s “whose fan is in his hand, and he will throughly purge his floor” (Matthew 3:12), with the week’s pillars by name, the doors of the Wards that hid someone and a verse for each, and the Stones paste the Tide Table over it by Vespers.

In the Wards a Thresher is a flail, and on a march day the keyed of the Wards keep indoors and the doors shut, and Old Nod opens its windows and sings back. The Charter Wardens at the gates could turn the Threshers back and never have, and the Second Fold count every pillar a march has made.

A Thresher bleaches the parish white until it rasps, and the band of sacking is the only rough thing on them, bound tight enough to raise a weal the Terraces admire. The flails are rinsed in a trough of salt water at the Threshing Floor after every march, and the marchers keep the step of the psalm in ruled ranks, every back the same white, so the Street Between hears them as one foot.

A household that shelters a keyed son behind a parish wall knows the Tellers by the brightness of a neighbor’s halo, which rises every Lord’s Day while the household waits. The Wards call a sudden brightening on a landing a Teller’s week.

The Tellers believe they are saving the Wards a door at a time, and their Grace climbs as the stairwells empty. A Teller’s children are taught that a neighbor salted is a neighbor spared worse, and a Teller who has given witness against her own kin wears a white sash in the hall for the twenty Grace it paid. The Wards paint a rat over the door of every Teller they find, and the Teller takes it as a badge.

A Teller looks like nobody by design, plain white kept spotless and a face that nods on every landing, and the craft lives in the counting, stairs, footfalls and breath, kept in the head like a rosary. The best of them kneel at the turn of the stair at Compline with eyes shut and lips moving, and the household above takes it for prayer until the Wardens come up the same stairs.

Faithful who read the verse the Covenant leaves out: “And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring” (John 10:16). Some of the near ripe come to them in the weeks before a Gleaning, frightened, and the Second Fold teach them dimming, the slow way down from nine hundred. Grace falls with every desire the Hum does not approve (Directive 3:4), so a soul that dims keeps one small want, a sweet or a song, and confesses enough small ones that every fall has a confession to match, and sits at eight hundred and eighty, since a confession brings back part of what a want cost and never all of it. The parishes say the Second Fold have kept more souls from the Gleanings than the Gleaners have.

The parishes despise them more than the keyed, since a goat cannot help being a goat and a Second Folder chooses the goats, and the Threshers’ Fan prints every one salted under the heading SHEEP’S CLOTHING, after the false prophets “which come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves” (Matthew 7:15). The Wards love them and never quite trust them, and Pan says they are the only sealed in Nodus worth saving from the Supper.

The Second Fold wear the parish white like everyone and hide one stain in it, a thread of color sewn into the inside hem, a sweet’s wrapper folded in a sleeve, the small daily desire that keeps them dim enough to stay. They wash the wounds of the keyed with bare hands in the stairwells, and a Deacon who finds blood under a Second Folder’s nails at confession knows exactly whose it is.

The family sings its shifts in white with sleeves pinned back, and the neighbors bring lilies to the doorway and come back each week to see how bright the face has grown.

Faithful who read the margins.
