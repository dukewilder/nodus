THE STONES // HELD BY THE CAINITES

Old Nod

SHOW ON THE MAP

The oldest ground in the city, above the old harbor on the west shore of the bay: narrow streets that follow no plan anyone has found, and magenta light. It was the harbor town before the charter, and on the night of the Parousia more of its windows burned on than in all the rest of the city together. CAM 07 stood on a mast above one of its roofs.

The city calls it the Stones, after the black stones the crews found under the harbor when they dug the cable vaults. Within a year of the charter most of it belonged to the Cainites, who had kept the location of Nod for eighteen hundred years and came to live on top of it. Every cable laid into Nodus runs through the Stones.

THE OPEN

A shop on the corner of two lanes in the middle of the Stones that sold everything and never closed. Dora Pell kept it, as her mother had before the charter. Stonecutter owned the building, and in the year of the Leak it put a generator in the cellar of every house it had; she took hers for the freezers. On the night of the Parousia her window was the brightest in the city, and the sign in it said OPEN. The city has called the shop the Open since.

Half the Stones came to it before dawn. The money had died with the grid and the till with it, and the accounts of what she did about that disagree: some say she gave the shelves away, and some that she took what people had in their pockets. She says she wrote every name in a book, and that most of them still owe her. Twice since, the Pillar has stopped in the lane outside and stood there for the length of an Hour, and both times the Open stayed open.

THE TIDE TABLE

It gives the hours at which the Pillar is expected in each street, the price of a charged battery, and the missing and the dead, the dead marked died unread where they did. It has printed no indexed name since the fourth year. Every week it is pasted up on the walls of the Stones over the week before.

OLD NOD // THE QUAYS

THE UNION HALL

The hall of the Harbor and Freight Union, which the crews who dug the cable vaults founded in the charter’s first year, and which the dockers, the drivers and the crews of the cable houses joined after them. In P-8 it stopped the port for twenty-three days over the night shift, and the Company settled. The roll of its members is painted on the long wall, a name to a line, and read aloud at the start of every meeting.

It still meets, sealed and keyed together. At every Hour the meeting stops while the sealed members kneel on the boards and the others wait with their caps in their hands. By its rules a member is a member until death, and the salted are not dead, so their names are read with the rest and their dues are waived. Since the Harvest it has read the names of the taken as well, and nobody answers to them. It bargains now with the Exchange for the boats that go out through the Breach, and its members say the Exchange is the first employer they have had that reads them the terms twice.

NOD

THE SPIKE // CONTESTED

Babel

SHOW ON THE MAP

Babel Exchange, the tallest building on Earth and the reason Nodus matters. Every cable that comes ashore at the Landing ends in its vault, and before the Parousia most of the planet’s traffic passed within two kilometers of it. Its beacon was the first light in the city to die at the Parousia, and in the whiteout that followed the light broke its crown and the top forty floors with it. One hundred and eighty-six floors still stand, and at the Parousia the Wheel took station beside them.

Since P+9 the front line of the war has run vertically through it. Heaven holds the upper floors from the broken crown down, the Tares hold the vault and the lower floors where the cables are, and between them lies the Babel Line, a few floors of burned slab that move up or down by the week.

THE WAITING ROOM

The waiting room of the clinic behind the CLINIC sign, carried down the stairs into Chrome Row Station with the rest of the clinic in the weeks after the Parousia: twelve chairs, a desk and a book. Maren Holt kept the clinic’s appointments for ten years on a screen. On the morning after the Parousia she came in at eight as she always had, found the screen dead, and wrote the day out on paper from memory. Twenty-six patients came, and none of them for the appointment they had. They came to ask whether what was in them would still work. It did.

She has kept the book since, and she writes down whatever name a patient gives her. The woman who shared the desk with her took the Seal in the first year and keeps the book of the Mercy clinics now, in one of the halls of Zion Heights, in the same neat hand.

The Exchange sells what is left by the shelf. Nobody has come for the hand.
