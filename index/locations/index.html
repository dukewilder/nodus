Old Nod

SHOW ON THE MAP

The oldest ground in the city, above the old harbor on the west shore of the bay, with narrow streets that follow no plan anyone has found, and magenta light. The narrowest of them, in the west of the district, are called the Narrows. It was the harbor town before the charter, and on the night of the Parousia more of its windows burned on than in all the rest of the city together. Every line in it is keyed, and its windows burn all night. CAM 07 still stands on its mast above one of its roofs.

The city calls it the Stones, after the black stones the crews found under the harbor when they dug the cable vaults. Within a year of the charter most of it belonged to the Cainites, who had kept the location of Nod for eighteen hundred years and came to live on top of it. Every cable laid into Nodus runs through the Stones.

The Unsigned live in the Narrows, the Psalters write out the week’s hymns on the quays, and the families of the line sit on the Step by day. The Stones are the ground of the Fowlers, the Forgers and the Ridden, the Tide Table is printed on the quays, Lazarus keeps its first station behind a door in the Stones, Lintel’s Dooryard hangs the parishes’ old doors on the Stones’ houses, and the Naming House keeps its door open on the Street Between. The Union’s pigeons fly home to the Loft on the customs house, the Mission keeps its nine rooms on the quays, and under the Stones the Lot sits at the Weir.

The graffiti of Old Nod is magenta and cyan on grey, and it covers every wall that faces Zion Heights. The commonest is Michael’s name translated and answered, WHO IS LIKE GOD? I AM. The rest is inverted stars, drawn by hand and never twice the same, and they mark where the Throne’s sight ends.

THE OPEN

A shop on the corner of two lanes in the middle of the Stones that sells everything and has never closed. Dora Pell keeps it, as her mother did before the charter. Stonecutter owns the building, and in the year of the Leak it put a generator in the cellar of every house it had; she took hers for the freezers. On the night of the Parousia her window was the brightest in the city, and the sign in it said OPEN. The city has called the shop the Open since.

Half the Stones came to it before dawn. The money had died with the grid and the till with it. Some say she gave the shelves away, and some that she took what people had in their pockets. She says she wrote every name in a book, and that most of them still owe her. Twice since, the Pillar has stopped in the lane outside and stood there for the length of an Hour, and both times the shop kept its door open.

Dora Pell sells plaster Lambs from Graven Lane at the till, a dozen to the battery, and keeps one on the counter with its head off for the sealed who come in.

Dora Pell pierces an ear or a nipple at the till for a battery with the headless Lamb looking on, and the sealed who come in for candles study the ceiling.

THE TIDE TABLE

The harbor’s news sheet, older than the charter, runs to two pages a week, printed on a treadle press at the back of a chandler’s on the quays, with the tides on the front and the fish prices, the funerals and the weddings on the back. The city of the charter read everything on screens, and the Tide Table went on printing for the boats.

It is the only news anyone in Nodus has printed since the Parousia, and it still puts the tides first, since the boats go out through the Breach on the ebb. It gives the hours at which the Pillar is expected in each street and the curfew map, which Heaven publishes by Directive, the Exchange’s rate for a battery, Darkwell’s rate for a charge and the chargers’ rate, and the missing and the dead, the dead marked died unread where they did. It has printed no indexed name since the fourth year. Every week it is pasted up on the walls of the Stones over the week before.

The back page has run the same classified since the first year, WANTED, ONE GOD, MUST BE HOUSETRAINED, WILL BE GUTTED ON ARRIVAL, APPLY THE STONES, beside adverts for horn fittings and chrome jaws. Before each anniversary the Supper goes in among the tides as HIGH WATER 03:12, ALL BREAD TO BE ON THE SLAB, and the sealed tear that corner off before they carry it home.

THE COLD STORE

A club in the harbor’s old cold store, where the boats froze their catch before the charter. THE UNION HALL

The hall of the Harbor and Freight Union, which the crews who dug the cable vaults founded in the charter’s first year, and which the dockers, the drivers and the crews of the cable houses joined after them. In P-8 it stopped the port for twenty-three days over the night shift, and the Company settled. The roll of its members is painted on the long wall, a name to a line, and read aloud at the start of every meeting.

It stands in the Stones off the quays’ rolls, so it never became a parish hall, and its sealed members cross to it by day. It still meets, sealed and keyed together. At every Hour the meeting stops while the sealed members kneel on the boards and the others wait with their caps in their hands. By its rules a member is a member until death, and the salted are not dead, so their names are read with the rest and their dues are waived. Since the Harvest it has read the names of the taken as well, and nobody answers to them. It bargains now with the Exchange for the boats that go out through the Breach, and its members say the Exchange is the first employer they have had that reads them the terms twice.

The banner of the first winter shows a docker with chrome hooks for hands tearing the Wheel open like a crab, and the words AN INJURY TO ONE IS AN INJURY TO ALL, SO GUT THE FARMER. Every meeting moves to strike Heaven from the roll of employers, and it carries, the sealed praying on their knees that every hand raised be taken off at the wrist.

The Pews call the dead US the Lord’s correction, the only one Old Nod has ever let Him make.

While the sealed sellers kneel among the crates at Lauds, the keyed fishwives beside them open mackerel with chrome nails and cry the whole Hour to the tune of the Sanctus, holy, holy, holy, two batteries the string.

THE CHARGERS

The bonded sheds of the customs house, a row of brick warehouses on the quays where the chargers have filled batteries in the dark since the first weeks after the Parousia, among racks of them, each on a keyed line, at a rate the Tide Table prints every week. Anyone in Nodus who lights a room on a battery has had it filled here or somewhere like it. Under the charter the harbor’s goods waited on their duty in the sheds. The Union’s pigeons live in the Loft on the customs house roof above them.

On the night of the Parousia the sheds held a ship’s cargo of batteries waiting on its duty, and by the first noon the harbor had carried off every one. The chargers keep one battery in five, one in four at the neaps, so a household brings five empty and takes four home full, and the fifth, filled, is the shed’s to sell. The Union’s drivers, who carry the full ones out to the Wards on foot, keep one battery in ten of those they carry. The Wards call the chargers’ fifth robbery. The Deacons teach that a room lit by such a battery is a room the Lord has stopped looking into, and the Wards buy them for that.

THE CLOSETS
