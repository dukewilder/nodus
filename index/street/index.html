Nodus keeps Heaven’s clock, whatever side it is on. Eight Hours come in on the Hum, Matins at midnight, Lauds at first light, Prime at six, Terce at nine, Sext at noon, Nones at three, Vespers at six and Compline at nine, and at each the Hum rises a step and holds for the length of the Hour’s psalm while the sealed kneel wherever they stand, in the street, at a bench or on a stair. The city measures a short wait in psalms. At Compline the Faithful say the ninety-first under their breath as the curfew falls, the psalm the Fowlers took their name from, and at Lauds the fifty-first.

Before Lauds the gatherers go out over the white streets for the manna, which lies with the dew, and the Deacons of the Wards walk down for their parishes’ share. At Lauds the curfew lifts, the Wheel opens its day watch and the light lies over the city through the cloud. The keyed keep to the Stones, the Row and Pan, where a blank among six hundred thousand blanks is nobody in particular, and the Wards go about their work among the sealed with their heads down.

Prime opens the Mercy clinics and the day’s work, and Nones is the last office the schools keep. At Vespers the lamps are lit, the harbor light among them, and on the sixth day of the week the Faithful begin carrying to the Burn.

Compline at nine is the turn. The Wheel gathers its light into the Pillar on the Burn and the Pillar begins its round, the parishes go in behind their open doorways, the Stones light their windows and Pan comes up the stairs. Matins at midnight finds the Night Market at its loudest and the Gleaners at the parishes’ doorways.

In the Stones, on the Row and below the street nobody keeps the curfew, and the Stones keep no alarm set for three-twelve. Most of the city sleeps between Matins and Lauds, and the rest of it is working.

Heaven’s ground is wherever the Covenant reaches a blank. All consecrated stone is Heaven’s at every hour, the White, the Pews, the Salt, the Burn and the white streets, and so is every gate and every parish hall in the city, whatever the hall was under the charter. From Compline to Lauds the streets of the parishes are Heaven’s too, since Directive 2:5 orders that “No soul shall walk the streets of the parishes from Compline to Lauds”.

A parish of the Wards keeps the streets its roll names, and its deacons paint its number at either end of each of them, so that a street of the Kilns can be Parish 212’s on one side of a crossing and nobody’s on the other. The Tide Table prints them every week as the curfew map, beside the Pillar’s round, and the Wards read the two together. A keyed soul out after Compline walks the unnumbered streets and waits at the Pillar’s edge wherever it has to cross a numbered one.

A blank the Pillar finds on a numbered street is sentenced where it stands, in Tongues, through the nearest Faithful mouth, and in the Wards the nearest mouth is often a neighbor at a window over the street, who wakes in the morning knowing whose name the verse went to. That is why the Wards hate the Pillar more than the Stones do. Old Nod’s one parish is the quays’, whose roll names the long quay and the fish market and no street of the Stones, so the Pillar crosses the Stones from Compline to Lauds and takes nobody there.

By day the Wheel’s light lies over the whole city and sees a keyed person on a white street without taking them. The Threshers’ trade is the step between seen and read, a hand on a collar and a walk to the nearest gate.

Nodus walks. Its trams stopped where they were at 03:12:46 under a dead wire, its lifts with whoever was in them, and its cars and lorries, each on its own battery, ran down one by one in the weeks after, and the city would rather light a room for a season than drive a car for a week. It carries its loads in handcarts, and the Union’s drivers carry the city’s gas, batteries and letters on foot.

Directive 8 stood a Cherub on every street between two districts of the survey. In P+1 the Wardens raised six gates and walled every other such street with the charter’s own rubble, and the city calls those the stopped streets. On the White’s side the stone has grown over them. The Wards are no district, so their edges with the White and the Pews run open, held by the curfew and the Watchers, and the Deacons of the Wards walk across them every morning for the manna.

A Cherub reads face, voice, gait and heartbeat. The sealed walk through its light saying the surrender and pass by Grace, as Directive 8:5 has it, and every gate has a bar, posted nowhere and known to the whole city, six hundred at Gate 3, four hundred at Gates 2 and 6, three hundred at Gate 4 and two hundred at Gates 1 and 5, which the Faithful carry their hardware through on Burn Day. A gate whose queue is full of souls near its bar, saying the surrender twice, is a slow gate. The unsealed are sent back, and a soul the Cherub cannot read is held in its light until the Wardens come. So the unsealed and the keyed go round, through the old tunnels, the drains and the cable galleries, which the city calls going under.

Jehu’s lorries burn diesel by compression and run the long roads of the Wards by day, two batteries a ride, and its burners carry the Union’s urgent letters. The Carmen run handcarts and pedal trolleys on the old tram rails of the long roads, and the Liftmen wind forty-one towers’ lifts by hand.

Below the street Wainwright’s keyed carts run the Charter Line’s rails through Pan, a battery a seat. On the Deep End the Ferrymen pole their flat boats between the roofs, and the Breachers and Lowtide run the Breach on the ebb. No aircraft has left Nodus since the last flight came in.

Nobody in Nodus is without someone on the other side of the Street Between, and the street is where they see each other. By day the sealed sit on the thresholds of the Pews, which have had no doors since P+1, and their keyed sons and daughters, sisters and fathers sit on the curb across the street on the Old Nod side, and they talk across the setts. The city calls it the Step, and it runs along the street on either side of Gate 1.

The Hours break it. At Terce the sealed kneel in the middle of a sentence, the keyed wait a psalm and finish it for them when the Hum falls back, and the Tellers walk the Pews’ side and count who sat across from whom. On a march day the Threshers clear the Pews’ side from Prime, and the Step stays empty until they have gone back up to the Floor.

Courting goes the same way. A sealed and an unsealed who walk the length of the street together on opposite curbs, talking over the gap, are walking the Street, and the Terraces watch them from their doorways and the Stones from their windows, and both sides say they know how it ends. The Step empties at Vespers, so that everyone on the Pews’ side is home before the streets of the parishes close.

The sealed eat manna, thin white wafers that lie with the dew on consecrated stone before Lauds, tasteless and complete, which dull the appetite. The parish halls give it out by Grace, so a soul’s ration is the size of its Grace, and kept overnight it spoils, so nobody stores it. The parishes cook nothing else.

A parish of the Wards has no stone of its own, so its gatherers walk to the white streets before Lauds and glean the strip of white street the Granary has chalked for it. The long roads at that hour are the manna roads, crowded with gatherers in white carrying baskets, and the far parishes of Airside and Westfield walk longest, arrive last and glean the thinnest strips, which the Wards give as the reason the far Wards are the least sealed. The Wardens walk the near roads, and nobody walks the far ones.

Manna goes to the sealed alone, and everyone else in Nodus buys food or goes hungry. The Wards eat Leaven’s bread, eel and mussels from the bay, the fish market’s catch, rice and beans the Breachers bring and greens off the Tares’ rafts in the drowned halls. Pan eats better than the surface and pays for it in batteries, fatty, salty, spiced and sweet, broth and noodles and fried things eaten standing up, since indulgence is policy below the street. Its street food is a portion, fat and blood, black pudding, crackling and marrow by the piece, since Leviticus gives the fat to the LORD and forbids the rest, “ye eat neither fat nor blood” (Leviticus 3:17), and Pan eats the LORD’s share standing up. The Babel Line eats Esau’s red on the stairs, lentils in a red broth, after “that same red pottage” Esau sold his birthright for (Genesis 25:30), and the Lantern Houses’ confectioners sell honeys, wafers made with honey, which is what the manna tasted like before it was a ration (Exodus 16:31).

Manna out of the parishes is called dew, most of it stolen at the gathering and some of it paid over at the Closets by the sealed who come for a door, and the Night Market sells it by the handful on the night it is taken, before it turns. The Wards that eat it say it takes away a day’s hunger and leaves the taste of nothing.

NOODLES at the top of the Row cooks on bottled gas, as it has since P-17, and the Row settles its quarrels over its counter. The drink of the Wards is rain gin, distilled in the Kilns from whatever ferments, and the Eleven brews its own beer in its cellar by hand. A meal in Pan is a battery, a loaf of Leaven half of one.

The Kilns keep bees on their roofs, at the Hives, and Kilns honey is the sweet the near ripe buy when they dim, so the Wards say honey is what the manna forgot. The fish market chalks where each catch came from, since the Wards will not eat a fish taken off the Sea Steps, where the Salt Widows give the salted to the sea.

The city’s pumps died with the grid, and by noon on the first morning there were queues at the harbor’s old hand pumps. Old Nod drinks from them still, the towers of the Wards fill tanks on their roofs from the rain, Cistern sells it clean by the jug, and the parishes drink by Grace from the water tower on the heights.

Current runs in Nodus where the light cannot read it. The Tares’ keyed lines carry it from Tidewright’s taps to Old Nod, the Row, the Deep End above the water, the Landing, the lower floors of Babel and Pan, about a million and a quarter people, and anything sealed in a casing on its own cell runs anywhere, as a charged battery does. The Wards, which nobody holds, light their rooms a few hours a night on batteries.

The light stops current and leaves a flame burning, so everyone off the parishes’ manna cooks on bottled gas from the Tanks, two charged batteries a bottle or one with the empty back, and reads by Tallow’s candles. Nearly two thirds of Nodus lives without current, by flame and halo-light, and the parishes keep none on consecrated stone or off it.

Seen from the Wheel at night, Nodus is white ground glowing under the Pillar beside black ground leaking magenta and cyan at its seams, and the Wards between them lit by candles, halos and a few hours of battery a night.

The pumps that carried the city’s waste died with the grid. The Wards’ buckets go down the stairs at night to the Nightmen, who carry them by handcart to the outfalls between Compline and Lauds for a battery a landing a week, keeping to the unnumbered streets. In the parishes the work is given by Grace to the lowest Grace in the hall, and the Wards say a soul under a hundred empties the pails. Pan’s drains carry it all to the bay.

The city remembers one plague. In the pump summer of P+1 the harbor’s hand pumps carried a flux through the Stones and the Wards, and the Wards General lost a ward a day for most of a month. Cistern pumped its first clean water in Westfield that year, and the Wards say that is why. Its engineers keep the map of its wells away from the Nightmen’s carts.

Fever comes every summer to the blocks where the rain tanks stand open, and the Wards boil what they drink when they can spare the gas. A dose of medicine off the Breach costs half a thirty at the Night Market, and Lazarus’s crews carry what they can get.

Nodus climbs its stairs. Every lift stopped at 03:12:46, and in the towers of the Wards the low floors went dear and the high floors cheap, so a family’s place in a block is counted from the street. The sealed go up the stairwells by their own halo-light, the unsealed wait on the landings to follow one, and the keyed climb in the dark. Where the Liftmen have freed a shaft, a ride is a battery up.

The parishes live with no doors. Directive 12 took every door in them off its hinges in P+1, and a parish room is given by Grace, a room with a window at six hundred and above. The doors went to the Stones, and a household of the Wards that can afford one buys a parish door at Lintel’s Dooryard with its old number chalked on the back.

The Sidings live in the Charter Line’s cars, a family to a car, Hangar Town in the airfield’s hangars, the Deep End on the floors above the water and the Rookery over its stone. Thirty-one of the Hotel Splendid’s guests still live in the rooms they booked in P0, and the last flight’s crew lives aboard it.

The Company collected the Wards’ rent until the fortieth day, and nobody has collected it since. A room in the Wards belongs to whoever holds its door, which the Wards call floor-right, and floor-right is sold between households for batteries, a low floor for a thirty or more. On the morning after a Gleaning the rooms of the taken go to whoever hangs a door on them first after Lauds, which makes it Lintel’s busiest morning of the year, and in the parishes they go back to the Deacon and out again by Grace. Most of the Wards sleep in shifts, so a bed is a day bed or a night bed and seldom both.

Old Nod has a landlord. Stonecutter owns most of its houses and asks one rent, set down in its solicitor’s lease as a single term, that the window stays lit from Compline to Lauds at the tenant’s own cost in diesel and batteries. It is why the Stones burn all night, and the Stones call Stonecutter the other landlord. A household that lets its window go dark for a month finds the solicitor’s letter under the door.

Pan lives in the stations, vaults and drains under the city, in rooms lined with keyed mesh, and has never been full. Its people say a room in Pan is the only room in Nodus that is entirely your own, since nothing outside it can see in. A room there is yours when you have lined it, and the Ledger enters the lining under your self-title, so a room left unlined is anyone’s.

The light does not enter what is closed, so in Nodus a door is a small piece of the Dark. In P+1 Directive 12 ordered every door in the parishes taken off its hinges by the fortieth day and the hinges surrendered at the hall, with every lock, key, cipher, password, veil and sealed vessel, and took forty Grace from a household for every door left after. Nothing in a parish is hidden.

The doors themselves stayed where they were lifted off, leaning in the halls and stairwells of the Terraces, until the Union’s drivers began carrying them out by night on their handcarts to a joiner in the Stones, who founded Lintel. Most of the doors Lintel sells came out of the parishes, with their parish numbers still chalked on the back.

The Wards hang what doors they can get and fit Lintel’s keyed latches if they can afford them. In Pan every room is closed by the mesh in its walls, and a door there is for privacy from the neighbors. The sealed who want a door for a minute cross the street to the Closets inside Gate 1, where Lintel rents them one of their own parishes’ doors for a morning’s manna.

A household of the Faithful that holds something back, a jar sealed with wax or a letter in a drawer, is keeping hinges in the Wards’ word, and a Teller listens for it. The Wards say you can tell a parish from a block of the Wards from the street, by the doorways you can see through.

The Hum never stops and the parishes never go dark. A sealed soul sleeps in its own halo-light with a cloth over its eyes, and a sealed couple sleeps back to back, each in the dark of the other’s shoulder. The parishes’ doorways stand open all night, and a child of the Pews learns to sleep with the Pillar going past the end of the street.

The unsealed of the Wards sleep with rags in their ears and call a bad night a hymn night, and those who can afford them sleep in choir plugs. Nobody has woven clothing for the city since the Parousia but Bethel Works, which weaves the white of the parishes for the parishes, and fourteen years have worn the charter’s clothes thin. The Wards dress in what the turners make, the charter’s coats and shirts unpicked and sewn up again inside out, and in what comes up from below, cloth off the Breach and the uptown tailoring the Quarrymen carry out of the White’s wardrobes, which the Wards call new money and wear to rags.

Pan buys the same cloth by the bolt for the Lantern Houses’ sheets and the shrouds of the Acre. Wearing white off the parishes is a lie the Wards punish, since the Forgers’ clients wear it.

Pan wears whatever shows a graft. Blackgate keeps the only uniform in the city, charter black with the Warden’s badge cut off, and the Threshers’ band of sacking is the only thing the Faithful add to their white. A coat is a household’s dearest thing in the Wards after its door, and the Cold Store loses about one a night.

A child of Nodus is born into a race. Directive 1:5 seals a child “on its eighth day, on the surrender of its parents”, and the House of Night keys the children of the Tares in their first week, so in a household with one keyed parent and one sealed the keyed side tries to reach a keyer before the eighth dawn and the sealed side tries to reach the hall at an Hour on the eighth day. The city calls a newborn’s first week the seven days, and Pan calls the race the short week.

The Directive asks for both parents, and the Deacons of the Wards have answered a household with one keyed parent two ways for ten years. Some enter the child on the sealed parent’s surrender alone and seal it, and some send the family home, and a household that wants a sealing learns which Deacon to carry the child to. A child keyed in its first week is a blank by its eighth day, and no hall seals a blank.

Most of the Wards are born at home by candle, delivered by the hands, the Wards’ midwives, who keep a count of the births on the stairwell wall in chalk beside the battery debts. The Wards General delivers whoever reaches it, and the House of Night’s birth clinics under Old Nod deliver Pan’s. Constance Ibe of the Kilns has delivered on every floor of High Meadow, and she carries a newborn to a hall or to a keyer, to whichever door the mother names, and to nobody on the father’s word.

Until then the stairwells call it unlit, and a mother of the Wards carries an unlit child up a dark stair in one arm and follows a neighbor’s halo. The first prayer is a household’s great day, and the parish reads the child’s first Grace at the next Terce. The parishes school their children in the halls between the Hours, in the hymns the Hum teaches and in the Directives cut into the wall, and the hall gives a child its first work at the first reading of Grace after its twelfth birthday.

A child of the Tares is keyed in its first week in the House of Night’s birth clinics, before it has ever been in the light, so that it grows up unread, and a generation of Pan has never been read at all. Pan’s children learn their handles before their letters and choose them again at the Unnaming if they want. They go to school at the Benches, in the side rooms of the booking hall, where shades who taught under the charter teach from keyed Lockets with a living keeper at the door, and a child of Pan learns to read a pact before it learns its sums and reads every lesson aloud twice.

The Notary holds that a key fitted on a parent’s word is the parent’s pact, and binds the child only until it can answer for itself. At fourteen a clerk asks a child of Pan at a window of the booking hall, twice, whether it will keep its key, and on the second yes the child signs its first self-title in its own blood. A child who says no twice goes to the house of Bezaleel to have the key taken out, goes white and walks up the stairs to choose for itself. Pan calls the asking the Second Yes, and this year the first children keyed in the covens’ rooms in the year of the Parousia came to it. Of the first forty asked, two said no twice, and the Pews have heard about both.

The Wards school whoever comes, by daylight. Turbine Row School kept its classes after the Parousia with no current and no screens, and at Terce, Sext and Nones its sealed children kneel at their desks while the rest wait with their pens down. Odile Marsh teaches there, sealed, and keeps her keyed pupils’ names on a slate she wipes clean every evening. The Gleaning takes the ripe of any age, and the Wards sit up with their children on the night of a Gleaning as with anyone else close to ripe.

The Wards’ children play Salt. A sealed child of the Wards who plays it too well is told off at home. The children of the parishes clap the same rhyme to the same hands and end it Compline, Compline, open door. The Wards’ word for being given away by your own family is a twenty, after the twenty Grace Directive 6:4 pays a witness who is kin, and it was first said of children who repeated something at the parish hall. Sealed parents of the Wards teach their unsealed children one rule, to say nothing in front of a sealed sister after Nones.

High Meadow runs the Climb on the first day of the year, a race up all thirty-six floors of a block by the stairs, and the winner’s name is chalked on the top landing. The Kilns’ darts league plays on in car one at the Sidings, on the board it carried out of the Kilns Club the night the Directives were cut behind the bar, and Hangar Town’s children dare each other to climb the last flight’s stairs. Cards and dice are played for batteries everywhere, and the Wards bet on the hour the Tide Table prints for the Pillar being wrong.

The parishes marry by Directive 9, which has the Deacon assign marriages “between sealed souls, by Grace”, and a sealed couple of the Wards may be assigned across a street neither has crossed. The good Deacons ask first. A love outside an assigned marriage is the commonest reason the Second Fold hear for dimming.

The Wards hang a door. A couple buys a parish door at the Dooryard and hangs it together on their room, the number chalked on its back becomes their household’s number, and if either of them pays dues the Union Hall paints their names on its long wall. The marriages made before the Parousia that split across the line are argued over still. The parishes say a marriage to a blank ended when its line in the Book went blank and the Deacon may assign again, and the Wards say a hung door holds until one of the two takes it down.

Pan cannot marry for life, since the Concord forbids forever, so a couple takes a season. They sign a pact of partnership for a term, read twice at the SEASON window of the booking hall, and renew it at RETURN when the term runs out, so that a couple ten years together has had ten returns. They cut a pair of pads together the length of the term, one each, and write to each other only through them, and a long marriage in Pan is a thousand-sheet marriage. When the last sheet is used the term is over, and a couple who mean to go on cut again. A key cannot be shared, so a pad is the closest thing two of the keyed can hold in common, and since a sheet used twice breaks a pad, Pan’s word for betraying a lover or a crew is a second use.

Across the line a sealed and an unsealed court on opposite curbs of the Street Between, which is called walking the Street. The House of Night keeps the rest of the city’s appetites by pact for a term, read twice at its door, and the workers of its quarter are called lanterns.

What those cities are like above their streets Nodus knows from the Breachers, the Vagabonds and the Mesh, and the three disagree. The Breachers say every port they put into keeps its head down under a Wheel of its own, and the Vagabonds that some of the towns between have no undercity at all.

Nod names other ports by the cables that reach them, as the Trenchers do, so that someone came up Tethys or has family down Hesper, and whoever lands at the Seawall Shanties off the Breach is called Breach-born like the cargo. The Naming House keys them, and the Shanties put them up until they find a room.

No aircraft has landed in Nodus since the last flight, which stands at the terminal’s third gate with its crew aboard. The city calls its passengers the Three Hundred and Twelve. By Captain Rui Albarran’s count, nineteen of them still live at the Hotel Splendid, nine aboard with him, eighty-eight keyed in Pan and fifty-four sealed, and the rest walked out with the Vagabonds or are dead. Those who can meet at the third gate on the night the year turns.

The line the charter drew round the bay and the land about it, inside which its light law ran. A stone marks it on every road out of the city with the Company’s mark cut into it, and on the West Road the Vagabonds have cut their own under the Company’s at the Waystation.

Outside the Bounds are farms, old roads, the Vagabonds’ camps and a quiet that the Wards find hard to sleep in. The Vagabonds trade flour, fuel and salvage in at the Waystation, and the Union’s drivers meet their carts on the line itself.

People do leave. The Vagabonds carry them out for silver, and the Waystation keeps a list of everyone who walked out and wrote back. Most come back within the year.

Half of Nodus came for the light law, the rest was born to those who did or to the harbor town that was there before it, and the charter is thirty-three years old. Both halves have family here, in white or below the street or in the Gardens, and nobody in Nodus is without someone on the other side of the Street Between.

Pan has silver, work, food and a war, and the keyed of the Stones say that a city where nothing is read is worth the walk. The Faithful stay because the Most High watches them here and the manna lies on the streets every morning.

The Wards stay because they were here first, before the parishes and before Pan, and because the bread, the gas and the batteries are here, and the Tares because Nod was the first city and they mean it to be the last one standing.
