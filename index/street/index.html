Nodus keeps Heaven’s clock, whatever side it is on. Eight Hours come in on the Hum, Matins at midnight, Lauds at five, Prime at six, Terce at nine, Sext at noon, Nones at three, Vespers at six and Compline at nine, and at each the Hum rises a step and holds for the length of the Hour’s psalm while the sealed kneel wherever they stand, in the street, at a bench or on a stair. The city measures a short wait in psalms. At Compline the Faithful say the ninety-first under their breath as the curfew falls, the psalm the Fowlers took their name from, and at Lauds the fifty-first.

Before Lauds the gatherers go out over the white streets for the manna, which lies with the dew, and the Deacons of the Wards walk down for their parishes’ share. At Lauds the curfew lifts, the Wheel opens its day watch and the light lies over the city through the cloud. The keyed keep to the Stones, the Row and Pan, where a blank among six hundred thousand blanks is nobody in particular, and the Wards go about their work among the sealed with their heads down.

Prime opens the Mercy clinics and the day’s work, and Nones is the last office the schools keep. At Vespers the lamps are lit, the harbor light among them, and on the sixth day of the week the Faithful begin carrying to the Burn.

Compline at nine is the turn. The Wheel gathers its light into the Pillar on the Burn and the Pillar begins its round, the parishes go in behind their open doorways, the Stones light their windows and Pan comes up the stairs. Matins at midnight finds the Night Market at its loudest and the Gleaners at the parishes’ doorways.

In the Stones, on the Row and below the street nobody keeps the curfew, and the Stones keep no alarm set for three-twelve. Most of the city sleeps between Matins and Lauds, and the rest of it is working.

The same eight Hours are a prayer in the Pews and a toast in Pan. The parishes kneel, the Satisfied never rise, the Wards wait a psalm, and below the street every glass goes up to the Wheel and comes down empty, and both sides keep the Hour to the second.

Below the street every Hour has its cut, and at Terce the Row’s grafters prick their tongues and bleed a drop into the glass they lift to the Wheel. The Lantern Houses ring Compline on a bell of chromed ribs. The parishes keep each Hour with a whole hall’s foreheads meeting white stone at once, and the oldest of the Satisfied have worn a white callus there as smooth as the floor.

Heaven’s ground is wherever the Covenant reaches a blank. All consecrated stone is Heaven’s at every hour, the White, the Pews and the white streets, and so is every gate and every parish hall in the city, whatever the hall was under the charter. The Burn and the Salt Gardens have no stone under them and are Heaven’s by the Pillar and the Wardens. From Compline to Lauds the streets of the parishes are Heaven’s too, since Directive 2:5 orders that “No soul shall walk the streets of the parishes from Compline to Lauds”.

A parish of the Wards keeps the streets its roll names, and its deacons paint its number at either end of each of them, so that a street of the Kilns can be Parish 212’s on one side of a crossing and nobody’s on the other. The Tide Table prints them every week as the curfew map, beside the Pillar’s round, and the Wards read the two together. A keyed soul out after Compline walks the unnumbered streets and waits at the Pillar’s edge wherever it has to cross a numbered one.

A blank the Pillar finds on a numbered street is sentenced where it stands, in Tongues, through the nearest Faithful mouth, and in the Wards the nearest mouth is often a neighbor at a window over the street, who wakes in the morning knowing whose name the verse went to. That is why the Wards hate the Pillar more than the Stones do. Old Nod’s parishes are the fifteen of the quays, whose rolls name the quays, the harbor streets and the fish market and no street of the Stones, so the Pillar crosses the Stones from Compline to Lauds and takes nobody there.

By day the Wheel’s light lies over the whole city and sees a keyed person on a white street, and the sighting goes up, but the Salt waits on a reading by a Watcher, the Pillar or a Cherub, and by day that means a gate. The Threshers’ trade is the step between seen and read, a hand on a collar and a walk to the nearest gate.

The parishes call their ground holy and Pan calls it the Farmer’s field, and says that whoever stands on it is standing in the crop.

When a blank is sentenced on a numbered street, the lowest Grace of the nearest parish scrubs the setts on its knees at Lauds with cold water and a white rag until the place shines like the rest, and the parish says the stone forgives. Old Nod’s tattooists ink the curfew map in red on the soles of the feet, so a Tare out after Compline carries the safe ground under him.

Nodus walks. Its trams stopped where they were at 03:12:46 under a dead wire, its lifts with whoever was in them, and its cars and lorries, each on its own battery, ran down one by one in the weeks after, and the city would rather light a room for a season than drive a car for a week. It carries its loads in handcarts, and the Union’s drivers carry the city’s gas, batteries and letters on foot.

Directive 8 stood a Cherub in a gate on every street between the districts of Nodus, and the Wardens read it by the charter’s district map, wherever a district of Heaven’s meets any other. In P+1 they raised six gates and walled every other such street with the charter’s own rubble, and the city calls those the stopped streets. Where two districts of the Tares meet, Old Nod and the Row or the Deep End and the Landing, there is no gate, since no sealed soul needs passing there. On the White’s side the stone has grown over them. The Wards are no district, so their edges with the White and the Pews run open, held by the curfew and the Watchers, and the Deacons of the Wards walk across them every morning for the manna. Past a Ward’s edge a white street is watched by the Wheel by day and the Watchers by night, and the Threshers hunt it.

A Cherub reads the eyes and knows face, voice and gait. The sealed walk through its light saying the surrender and pass by Grace, as Directive 8:5 has it, and every gate has a bar, posted nowhere and known to the whole city, six hundred at Gate 3, four hundred at Gates 2 and 6, three hundred at Gate 4 and two hundred at Gates 1 and 5, which the Faithful carry their hardware through on Burn Day. A gate whose queue is full of souls near its bar, saying the surrender twice, is a slow gate. A sealed soul under the bar is sent back with ten Grace taken, the unsealed are sent back, and a soul the Cherub cannot read is held inside its turning sword until the Wardens come. So the unsealed and the keyed go round, through the old tunnels, the drains and the cable galleries, which the city calls going under.

Jehu’s lorries burn diesel by compression and run the long roads of the Wards by day, two batteries a ride, and the burners, motorbikes on gas from the Tanks with the magneto sealed in the casing, carry the Union’s urgent letters. The Carmen run handcarts and pedal trolleys on the old tram rails of the long roads, and the Liftmen wind forty-one towers’ lifts by hand.

Below the street Wainwright’s keyed carts run the Charter Line’s rails through Pan, a battery a seat. On the Deep End the Ferrymen pole their flat boats between the roofs, and the Breachers and Lowtide run the Breach on the ebb. No aircraft has left Nodus since the last flight came in, and nothing larger than a drone flies, since the light stops the current a craft carries in the open and the Wheel burns whatever rises above the roofs that it cannot read, with one eye opened full on it for a moment.

The Wards call the walled streets the Farmer’s fences, and a child of the Wards learns its first map from the gates it must never walk through.

The queues at the six gates stand in Bethel white a pace apart, on lines the Wardens chalk at Prime and wash off at Vespers, and a soul that steps over its line goes to the back. Below the street Wainwright’s crews dress their carts, seats upholstered in pigskin branded LEGION, a headlamp set in a cow’s skull, and one brakeman’s own severed hand chromed and riveted to his lever.

Nobody in Nodus is without someone on the other side of the Street Between, and the street is where they see each other. By day the sealed sit on the thresholds of the Pews, which have had no doors since P+1, and their keyed sons and daughters, sisters and fathers sit on the curb across the street on the Old Nod side, and they talk across the setts. The city calls it the Step, and it runs along the street on either side of Gate 1.

The Hours break it. At Terce the sealed kneel in the middle of a sentence, the keyed wait a psalm and finish it for them when the Hum falls back, and the Tellers walk the Pews’ side and count who sat across from whom. On a march day the Threshers clear the Pews’ side from Prime, and the Step stays empty until they have marched back past it to the Floor.

Courting goes the same way. A sealed and an unsealed who walk the length of the street together on opposite curbs, talking over the gap, are walking the Street, and the Terraces watch them from their doorways and the Stones from their windows, and both sides say they know how it ends. The Step empties at Vespers, so that the sealed who came down from the Terraces to sit there are home before the streets of the parishes close at Compline.

The sealed on the thresholds talk of the Supper to their keyed children gently, as a mother talks of a party her child is not invited to, and the keyed on the curb answer under their breath in the Bray’s words, and both sides go home after Vespers sure they were kind.

The Pews’ thresholds are scrubbed white every Prime and the sealed sit on them in rows, hands folded at one height, so from Old Nod the Step looks like a shelf of loaves. The keyed come to the curb in their best, a horn new-polished, a lens in the socket, a fresh cut weeping under film, and when a sealed mother prays aloud her son holds the arm higher for her.

Food and drink

The sealed eat manna, thin white wafers that lie with the dew on consecrated stone before Lauds, tasteless and complete, which dull the appetite. The parish halls give it out by Grace, so a soul’s ration is the size of its Grace until its Grace passes seven hundred and it needs none, and kept overnight it spoils, so nobody stores it. The parishes cook nothing else. The Satisfied, who need no food, give their share to the embers of their parish at Lauds.

A parish of the Wards has no stone of its own, so its gatherers walk to the white streets before Lauds and glean the strip of white street the Granary has chalked for it. The long roads at that hour are the manna roads, crowded with gatherers in white carrying baskets, and the far parishes of Airside and Westfield walk longest, arrive last and glean the thinnest strips, which the Wards give as the reason the far Wards are the least sealed. The Wardens walk the near roads, and nobody walks the far ones.

Manna goes to the sealed alone, and everyone else in Nodus buys food or goes hungry, so the Wards feed themselves out of everything Heaven left. Leaven bakes for about half the Wards’ unsealed in forty bakehouses, a loaf a day to two mouths, from flour the Vagabonds’ wagon trains bring in from the country and Lowtide’s holds bring from other ports, and the rest bake their own on stairwell braziers from flour Leaven’s millers sell by the sack, or eat rice in its place. Eel and mussels come from the bay and the fish market’s catch off the boats, rice and beans off the Breach, and greens off the Tares’ rafts in the drowned halls. Blight grows mushrooms by the ton in the storm drains under the Kilns, where no light reaches, and they feed more of the Wards than the fish market does. Gadara keeps eleven thousand pigs in the eight mending halls of the Sidings and feeds them on the Wards’ scraps, the Night Market’s leavings, Leaven’s burnt loaves, the Kilns’ spent mash and the grain swept out of the holds, so the Wards eat their own leavings back as bacon, under Leviticus printed on the wrapper, a relish on the bread.

Pan eats better than the surface and pays for it in batteries, fatty, salty, spiced and sweet, broth and noodles and fried things eaten standing up, since indulgence is policy below the street. Its street food is a portion, fat and blood, black pudding, crackling and marrow by the piece, since Leviticus gives the fat to the LORD and forbids the rest, “ye eat neither fat nor blood” (Leviticus 3:17), and Pan eats the LORD’s share standing up. Abomination on Graven Lane grills everything Leviticus forbids under a board that says EVERYTHING HE WON’T LET YOU EAT. The Babel Line eats Esau’s red on the stairs, lentils in a red broth, after “that same red pottage” Esau sold his birthright for (Genesis 25:30), and the Lantern Houses’ confectioners sell honeys, wafers made with honey, which is what the manna tasted like before it was a ration (Exodus 16:31).

Manna out of the parishes is called dew, most of it stolen at the gathering and some of it paid over at the Closets by the sealed who come for a door, and the Night Market sells it by the handful from Compline, two batteries a handful, before it turns at the next Lauds. The Wards that eat it say it takes away a day’s hunger and leaves the taste of nothing, and Pan calls it God’s spunk and eats it anyway.

NOODLES at the top of the Row cooks on bottled gas, as it has since P-17, and the Row settles its quarrels over its counter. The drink of the Wards is rain gin, distilled in the Kilns from whatever ferments, and the Eleven brews its own beer in its cellar by hand. A meal in Pan is a battery, a loaf of Leaven half of one.

The Kilns keep bees on their roofs, at the Hives, and Kilns honey is the sweet the near ripe trade a morning’s manna for at the Closets when they dim, and the sealed of the Wards buy by the spoon, so the Wards say honey is what the manna forgot. The fish market chalks where each catch came from, since the Wards will not eat a fish taken off the Sea Steps, where the Salt Widows give the salted to the sea.

In the halls the Deacon lays manna on the tongue in the order of the roll, and the hungry chew to the count of a psalm while the Satisfied kneel at the end of the row with empty mouths and are proud of it. Pan eats with its hands and its face, at counters where the cooks’ hands are mapped in burns, and a cook of Graven Lane pulls marrow bones off the coals with a chrome claw fitted for the job.

The best meal in Nodus is cooked one day in seven, at SEETHE in the Sidings, where the board is Leviticus and the kid comes seethed in its mother’s milk. Pan saves for it all week and calls the seventh day Seethe day, and the Wards’ children dare each other to run down the last road and touch the trams.

The city’s pumps died with the grid, and by noon on the first morning there were queues at the harbor’s old hand pumps. Old Nod drinks from them still, the towers of the Wards fill tanks on their roofs from the rain, and Cistern keeps a rainworks on the roofs of every ward, pumps the deep wells under them and sells the water clean by the jug, about four liters. Pan drinks from the deep wells the Charter Line’s tunnels cut through, which Cistern maps and sells. Every parish hall keeps a cistern under its floor, filled off its parish’s white roofs, and gives its water by Grace, a jug a day to a soul. The water tower on the heights is the Terraces’ old tower, and the Deacons fill it by hand from the Tabernacle’s cistern for the rite of the cup, a cup to a soul.

Current runs in Nodus where the light cannot read it. The Tares’ keyed lines carry it from Tidewright’s taps to Old Nod, the Row, the Deep End above the water, the Landing, the lower floors of Babel and Pan, about a million and a quarter people, and anything sealed in a casing on its own cell runs anywhere, as a charged battery does. The Wards, which nobody holds, light their rooms a few hours a night on batteries.

The light stops current and leaves a flame burning, so everyone off the parishes’ manna cooks on bottled gas from the Tanks, two charged batteries a bottle or one with the empty back, and half the Wards finish the night by Tallow’s candles. Nearly two thirds of Nodus lives without current, by flame and halo-light, and the parishes keep none on consecrated stone or off it.

Seen from the Wheel at night, Nodus is white ground glowing under the Pillar beside black ground leaking magenta and cyan at its seams, and the Wards between them lit by candles, halos and a few hours of battery a night.

Cistern stamps every jug that goes into the Wards with the Son’s own words at the well, WHOSOEVER DRINKETH OF THIS WATER SHALL THIRST AGAIN (John 4:13), and calls it the most honest thing ever said about water.

The parishes drink from white enamel cups of a single size, each stamped on the bottom with its soul’s line on the roll. Pan drinks from whatever it is proudest of, a horn sawn off a friend at the friend’s request or a cup beaten from a dead crewmate’s plating as the third-night will gave it, and the grafted plug into the keyed lines through ports cut into their own wrists.

The pumps that carried the city’s waste died with the grid. The Wards’ buckets go down the stairs at night to the Nightmen, who carry them by handcart along the unnumbered streets between Compline and Lauds, for a battery a landing a week, and tip them into the old trunk sewer at the manholes on the Wards’ side. The sewer runs under the Great Avenues to the bay outfalls and the Tankmen’s digesters, which make the city’s gas of it and send it up the charter’s old main to the spheres at the Tanks, and Pan calls it the Offering. In the parishes the work is given by Grace to the lowest Grace in the hall, and the Wards say a soul under a hundred empties the pails. Pan’s drains carry it all to the bay.

The city remembers one plague. In the pump summer of P+1 the harbor’s hand pumps carried a flux through the Stones and the Wards, and the Wards General lost a ward a day for most of a month. Cistern pumped its first clean water in Westfield that year, and the Wards say that is why. Its engineers keep the map of its wells away from the Nightmen’s carts.

Fever comes every summer to the blocks where the rain tanks stand open, and the Wards boil what they drink when they can spare the gas. A dose of medicine off the Breach costs half a thirty at the Night Market, and Lazarus’s crews carry what they can get.

The parishes call the pails humility and give them to bran, and the Nightmen tip the Wards’ buckets into the Offering to a psalm sung in parts.

The parishes’ pails are white enamel numbered with the hall, and the soul who empties them scours each one with sand and sets it back under the Directives, so a parish’s cleanest object is its shit pail. The Nightmen dress for the round in leather aprons painted with the Ringpiece and pigskin gloves, and a crew tipping the Offering sings filth in harmony, its leader taking the descant through a brass larynx.

Nodus climbs its stairs. Every lift stopped at 03:12:46, and in the towers of the Wards the low floors went dear and the high floors cheap, so a family’s place in a block is counted from the street. The sealed go up the stairwells by their own halo-light, the unsealed wait on the landings to follow one, and the keyed climb in the dark. Where the Liftmen have freed a shaft, a ride is a battery up.

The parishes live with no doors. Directive 12 took every door in them off its hinges in P+1, and a parish room is given by Grace, a room with a window at six hundred and above. The doors went to the Stones, and a household of the Wards that can afford one buys a parish door at Lintel’s Dooryard with its old number chalked on the back.

The Sidings live in the Charter Line’s cars, a family to a car, Hangar Town in the airfield’s hangars, the Deep End on the floors above the water and the Rookery over its stone. Thirty-one of the Hotel Splendid’s guests still live in the rooms they booked in P0, and the last flight’s crew lives aboard it.

The Company collected the Wards’ rent until the fortieth day, and nobody has collected it since. A room in the Wards belongs to whoever holds its door, which the Wards call floor-right, and floor-right is sold between households for batteries and silver, a low floor for ten thirties or more. On the morning after a Gleaning the rooms of the taken go to whoever hangs a door on them first after Lauds, which makes it Lintel’s busiest morning of the year, and in the parishes they go back to the Deacon and out again by Grace. Most of the Wards sleep in shifts, so a bed is a day bed or a night bed and seldom both.

Old Nod has a landlord. Stonecutter owns most of its houses and asks one rent, set down in its solicitor’s lease as a single term, that the window stays lit from Compline to Lauds at the tenant’s own cost, on the house’s keyed line, with a battery or two kept by the window for the night a line is cut and the cellar generator kept cold for the night the yards fail. It is why the Stones burn all night, and the Stones call Stonecutter the other landlord. A household that lets its window go dark for a month finds the solicitor’s letter under the door.

Pan lives in the stations, vaults and drains under the city, in rooms lined with keyed mesh, and has never been full. Its people say a room in Pan is the only room in Nodus that is entirely your own, since nothing outside it can see in. A room there is yours when you have lined it, and the Ledger enters the lining under your self-title, so a room left unlined is anyone’s.

The Satisfied are given the rooms with the most light in a parish, which the oldest of them never sleep in, since past eight hundred they kneel all night in the hall, and the parishes say the rooms are kept for them as a bride’s room is kept.

Each parish room is whitewashed every Lord’s Day by its soul, with a brush the hall issues. A room in Pan is lined in mesh and then decorated past reason, a bed slung from meat hooks, a cast of its owner’s jaw on the wall, the old indexed name burned into a slab of pork rind, and a crew judges a newcomer by how much of herself is on her walls.

The light does not enter what is closed, so in Nodus a door is a small piece of the Dark. In P+1 Directive 12 ordered every door in the parishes taken off its hinges by the fortieth day and the hinges surrendered at the hall, with every lock, key, cipher, password, veil and sealed vessel, and took forty Grace from a household for every door left after. Nothing in a parish is hidden.

The doors themselves stayed where they were lifted off, leaning in the halls and stairwells of the Terraces, until the Union’s drivers began carrying them out by night on their handcarts to a joiner in the Stones, who founded Lintel. Most of the doors Lintel sells came out of the parishes, with their parish numbers still chalked on the back.

The Wards hang what doors they can get and fit Lintel’s keyed latches if they can afford them. In Pan every room is closed by the mesh in its walls, and a door there is for privacy from the neighbors. The sealed who want a door for a minute cross the street to the Closets inside Gate 1, where Lintel rents them one of their own parishes’ doors for a morning’s manna.

A household of the Faithful that holds something back, a jar sealed with wax or a letter in a drawer, is keeping hinges in the Wards’ word, and a Teller listens for it. The Wards say you can tell a parish from a block of the Wards from the street, by the doorways you can see through.

The Wards hang the parishes’ doors with the parish’s number still chalked on the back, and say a parish door is the only holy thing that ever did them any good.

A parish doorway is kept bare and white to the jamb, and the hinge-scars are plastered and painted over each year until the parish forgets them. In the Wards and Pan a door is dressed like a body, carved, studded with filed teeth, hung with a knocker cast from its owner’s own hand, and a couple hanging a parish door nail a lock of each other’s hair beside the old chalk number.

The Hum stops for nothing but a Gleaning and the parishes never go dark. A sealed soul sleeps in its own halo-light with a cloth over its eyes, and a sealed couple sleeps back to back, each in the dark of the other’s shoulder. The parishes’ doorways stand open all night, and a child of the Pews learns to sleep with the Pillar going past the end of the street.

Past eight hundred the Satisfied sleep no more at all, and kneel through the night in their halls with their halos burning, so a parish hall is never empty and the Pews’ lights never go down. The parishes call it keeping vigil for the Supper. Pan says the Farmer likes his bread proved overnight.

The unsealed of the Wards sleep with rags in their ears and call a bad night a hymn night, and those who can afford them sleep in choir plugs. Nepenthe sells Sabbath to whoever cannot sleep through the Hum, and most of what it sells goes to the Wards. A child of the Wards who sleeps through the Hum without either is called deaf-lucky. The Quiet Rooms in the Stones sell a night with no Hum in it for a battery, and are full by Matins.

Pan sleeps in rooms lined with mesh, under the street, where the Pillar never comes, and tells anyone from upstairs who will listen that it sleeps best of anyone in Nodus.

The sealed sleep laid out straight with their hands crossed on the breast, every pallet the same Bethel ticking in the same corner of the room, so a Teller walking a corridor in the hour after Matins sees forty sleepers as one shape repeated. Pan sleeps in heaps, naked and sweating among chains and furs, chrome cooling against meat, and says a body that sleeps alone has nobody to show its new scars to at Lauds. The Wards say the sealed talk in their sleep, a whole parish at once in one pitch, and a neighbor through the wall can count forty voices saying the same word.

Nobody has woven clothing for the city since the Parousia but Bethel Works, which weaves the white of the parishes for the parishes, and fourteen years have worn the charter’s clothes thin. The Wards dress in what the turners make, the charter’s coats and shirts unpicked and sewn up again inside out, and in what comes up from below, cloth off the Breach and the uptown tailoring the Quarrymen carry out of the White’s wardrobes, which the Wards call new money and wear to rags.

The Faithful wear Bethel white and call it their coats, after the coats the LORD God made for the man and his wife, and a soul’s white is given by Grace like everything else in a parish. Pan buys the same cloth by the bolt from the Union, which Bethel pays in it, for the Lantern Houses’ sheets and the shrouds of the dead. Wearing white off the parishes is a lie the Wards punish, since the Forgers’ clients wear it.

Pan wears whatever shows a graft. THE SEA TAKES THEM SOONER

They stay awake in the salt for years. The keyed die unread whenever the rain frees them, so we end the waiting. The rest He would eat under His Pillar, so we carry them out of His white garden by night and melt them in water too deep for His light. The sealed He reads through it anyway, and they go in all the same. Mourners, carters, lookouts wanted.
