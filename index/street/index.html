A DAY BY THE HOURS

THE STREET

Nodus keeps Heaven’s clock, whatever side it is on. Eight Hours come in on the Hum, Matins at midnight, Lauds at first light, Prime at six, Terce at nine, Sext at noon, None at three, Vespers at six and Compline at nine, and at each the Hum rises a step and holds for the length of the Hour’s psalm while the sealed kneel wherever they stand, in the street, at a bench or on a stair. The city measures a short wait in psalms.

Before Lauds the gatherers go out over the white streets for the manna, which lies with the dew, and the Deacons of the Wards walk down for their parishes’ share. At Lauds the curfew lifts, the Wheel opens its day watch and the light lies over the city through the cloud. The keyed keep to the Stones, the Row and Pan, where a blank among six hundred thousand blanks is nobody in particular, and the Wards go about their work among the sealed with their heads down.

Prime opens the Mercy clinics and the day’s work, and None is the last office the schools keep. At Vespers the lamps are lit, the harbor light among them, and on the sixth day of the week the Faithful begin carrying to the Burn.

Compline at nine is the turn. The Wheel gathers its light into the Pillar on the Burn and the Pillar begins its round, the parishes go in behind their open doorways, the Stones light their windows and Pan comes up the stairs. Matins at midnight finds the Night Market at its loudest and the Gleaners at the parishes’ doorways.

In the Stones, on the Row and below the street nobody keeps the curfew, and the Stones keep no alarm set for three-twelve. Most of the city sleeps between Matins and Lauds, and the rest of it is working.

The sealed eat manna, thin white wafers that lie with the dew on consecrated stone before Lauds, tasteless and complete, which dull the appetite. The parish halls give it out by Grace, so a soul’s ration is the size of its Grace, and kept overnight it spoils, so nobody stores it. The parishes cook nothing else.

Manna goes to the sealed alone, and everyone else in Nodus buys food or goes hungry. The Wards eat Leaven’s bread, eel and mussels from the bay, the fish market’s catch, rice and beans the Breachers bring and greens off the Tares’ rafts in the drowned halls. Pan eats better than the surface and pays for it in batteries, fatty, salty, spiced and sweet, broth and noodles and fried things eaten standing up, since indulgence is policy below the street.

Manna out of the parishes is called dew, most of it stolen at the gathering and some of it paid over at the Closets by the sealed who come for a door, and the Night Market sells it by the handful on the night it is taken, before it turns. The Wards that eat it say it takes away a day’s hunger and leaves the taste of nothing.

NOODLES at the top of the Row cooks on bottled gas, as it has since P-17, and the Row settles its quarrels over its counter. The drink of the Wards is rain gin, distilled in the Kilns from whatever ferments, and the Eleven brews its own beer in its cellar by hand. A meal in Pan is a battery, a loaf of Leaven half of one.

THE STREET

WHERE PEOPLE LIVE

THE PARISHES

THE WARDS

Nodus climbs its stairs. Every lift stopped at 03:12:46, and in the towers of the Wards the low floors went dear and the high floors cheap, so a family’s place in a block is counted from the street. The sealed go up the stairwells by their own halo-light, the unsealed wait on the landings to follow one, and the keyed climb in the dark. Where the Liftmen have freed a shaft, a ride is a battery up.

The parishes live with no doors. Directive 12 took every door in them off its hinges in P+1, and a parish room is given by Grace, a room with a window at six hundred and above. The doors went to the Stones, and a household of the Wards that can afford one buys a parish door at Lintel’s Dooryard with its old number chalked on the back.

The Sidings live in the Charter Line’s cars, a family to a car, Hangar Town in the airfield’s hangars, the Deep End on the floors above the water and the Rookery over its stone. Thirty-one of the Hotel Splendid’s guests still live in the rooms they booked in P0, and the last flight’s crew lives aboard it.

Pan lives in the stations, vaults and drains under the city, in rooms lined with keyed mesh, and has never been full. Its people say a room in Pan is the only room in Nodus that is entirely your own, since nothing outside it can see in.

THE STREET

The city’s pumps died with the grid, and by noon on the first morning there were queues at the harbor’s old hand pumps. Old Nod drinks from them still, the towers of the Wards fill tanks on their roofs from the rain, Cistern sells it clean by the jug, and the parishes drink by Grace from the water tower on the heights.

Current runs in Nodus where the light cannot read it. The Tares’ keyed lines carry it from Tidewright’s taps to Old Nod, the Row, the Deep End above the water, the Landing, the lower floors of Babel and Pan, about a million and a quarter people, and anything sealed in a casing on its own cell runs anywhere, as a charged battery does. The Wards, which nobody holds, light their rooms a few hours a night on batteries.

The light stops current and leaves a flame burning, so everyone off the parishes’ manna cooks on bottled gas from the Tanks, two charged batteries a bottle or one with the empty back, and reads by Tallow’s candles. Nearly two thirds of Nodus lives without current, by flame and halo-light, and the parishes keep none on consecrated stone or off it.

Seen from the Wheel at night, Nodus is white ground glowing under the Pillar beside black ground leaking magenta and cyan at its seams, and the Wards between them lit by candles, halos and a few hours of battery a night.

A parish nurses its own sick in its hall, by Grace, and the Faithful age and die as people did before the charter, since they refuse the Tree. The Mercy clinics in the nine white halls of the Terraces cut implants out of the repentant and take them off the Tree, once, between Prime and Compline, and one patient in nine dies of the mercy.

The Wards General on the West Road has a thousand beds and runs on batteries charged in the dark. It takes silver or nothing and has treated nobody sealed since the first year. Lazarus carries its subscribers down to nine stations under the street for a thirty a year, and keys before the end any subscriber who asked for it in the subscription.

Below the street the houses of the Row fit grafts and mend them, and an implant answers only to the house that fitted it, so a graft is for life and so is its house. The House of Night keys the children of Pan in their first week, keeps the gene-work and the Tree, and Nepenthe makes the medicine, the sleep and the forgetting.

Most of the Tares were on the Tree before the Parousia and the Night Market keeps them on it, and a Tare who ages is a Tare who could not pay. The Orchard gives the Wards its windfalls free, which slow the aging between courses and walk nothing back.

THE STREET

THE PRICE

Everything the Dark gives is paid for, and Pan reads the price before it signs. An implant answers only to the house that fitted it, so a graft is for life and so is its house, and the Row’s houses have closed since the Parousia as houses do, a fitter dead on the stairs or a bench sold off for silver. The grafts they leave behind are orphans, and no other house can mend one. Marbas of the Forge can, at Chrome Row Station, at the Forge’s price.

A sense lent to a demon comes back at the end of its term with everything the demon felt through it. Some lenders say their own meals taste of nothing for a month after a year of someone else’s, and the Wards say they have gone flat. Others say they got back the best year they ever had, and they lend again. The Return sells what comes back by the hour, and its regulars, who lie down in its booths most nights, are lie-downs to the rest of Pan.

A rider’s term ends and the rider goes, and it leaves something in its host, a habit, a word or a taste for a food the host had never eaten. The Ridden call it a leaving and compare theirs in the Lantern Houses. Most of them keep their leavings, and a few pay the House of Night to take one back.

The rest is paid in the body. Vigil’s three days are paid for with three more in Sabbath, Nepenthe sells nobody two doses of Lethe in a month, and a Tare who ages is a Tare who could not pay for the Tree. A key that dies leaves its wearer read, so the keyed of Pan have their keys looked at on the Row once a year, as people saw a dentist under the charter.

Nodus walks. Its trams stopped where they were at 03:12:46 under a dead wire, its lifts with whoever was in them, and its cars and lorries, each on its own battery, ran down one by one in the weeks after, and the city would rather light a room for a season than drive a car for a week. It carries its loads in handcarts, and the Union’s drivers carry the city’s gas, batteries and letters on foot.

Between districts every street goes through a Cherub gate, which reads face, voice, gait and heartbeat. The sealed walk through it saying the surrender, everyone else is sent back, and a soul the Cherub cannot read is held in its light until the Wardens come. So the unsealed and the keyed go round, through the old tunnels, the drains and the cable galleries, which the city calls going under.

Jehu’s lorries burn diesel by compression and run the long roads of the Wards by day, two batteries a ride, and its burners carry the Union’s urgent letters. The Carmen run handcarts and pedal trolleys on the old tram rails of the long roads, and the Liftmen wind forty-one towers’ lifts by hand.

Below the street Wainwright’s keyed carts run the Charter Line’s rails through Pan, a battery a seat. On the Deep End the Ferrymen pole their flat boats between the roofs, and the Breachers and Lowtide run the Breach on the ebb. No aircraft has left Nodus since the last flight came in.

BEASTS AND WEEDS

THE STREET

Nodus kept its animals through the Parousia. The gulls went on working the fish market’s scraps on the first morning, and the rats went on living in the drains as the first crews came down into them. In Pan every stall of the Night Market keeps a cat, and the Lantern Houses breed them. The Wards say a cat asleep on a step sleeps on through the Pillar as the step does. The keyed who wait at its edge in the next doorway have watched it happen, and they say the cats of the Wards are the only things in Nodus the Pillar has no use for.

The Harvest took 2.1 million people in one night and left their dogs. Manna is given to the sealed alone, so the parishes had nothing to give a dog, and in the weeks after the Harvest the dogs of the taken came down the Lanes and the long roads into the Wards, and the Wards fed what they could. Most blocks of the Wards keep a few of those dogs’ grandchildren on the stairs.

The gardeners of the Salt Gardens walk the rows at Lauds with rags on poles to keep the gulls off the pillars. Since the Harvest the parishes have kept no animals, and the manna a household keeps overnight against the Directive “bred worms, and stank” by morning, as Exodus says (Exodus 16:20). The Union’s pigeons fly over all of it by day, from the Loft on the customs house, carrying notes.

Nothing grows on the white stone. Off it the city has gone to weed in fourteen years, grass between the stopped tramlines of the long roads, willow through the roofs of the Sidings’ sheds and darnel in every crack of the Stones, the weed of the parable Heaven named the Tares for. The Cainites leave the darnel standing, and the Terraces pull up whatever they find of it on their side of the Street Between.

The light does not enter what is closed, so in Nodus a door is a small piece of the Dark. In P+1 Directive 12 ordered every door in the parishes taken off its hinges by the fortieth day and the hinges surrendered at the hall, with every lock, key, cipher, password, veil and sealed vessel, and took forty Grace from a household for every door left after. Nothing in a parish is hidden.

The doors themselves stayed where they were lifted off, leaning in the halls and stairwells of the Terraces, until the Union’s drivers began carrying them out by night on their handcarts to a joiner in the Stones, who founded Lintel. Most of the doors Lintel sells came out of the parishes, with their parish numbers still chalked on the back.

The Wards hang what doors they can get and fit Lintel’s keyed latches if they can afford them. In Pan every room is closed by the mesh in its walls, and a door there is for privacy from the neighbors. The sealed who want a door for a minute cross the street to the Closets inside Gate 1, where Lintel rents them one of their own parishes’ doors for a morning’s manna.

A household of the Faithful that holds something back, a jar sealed with wax or a letter in a drawer, is keeping hinges in the Wards’ word, and a Teller listens for it. The Wards say you can tell a parish from a block of the Wards from the street, by the doorways you can see through.

A child of the Faithful is sealed at eight days on its parents’ surrender and grows its halo the first time it prays unassisted, and its Grace is read with the rest of the parish on the Lord’s Day. The parishes school their children in the halls between the Hours, in the hymns the Hum teaches and in the Directives cut into the wall.

A child of the Tares is keyed in its first week in the House of Night’s birth clinics, before it has ever been in the light, so that it grows up unread, and a generation of Pan has never been read at all. Pan’s children learn their handles before their letters, and choose them again at the Unnaming if they want.

The Wards school whoever comes, by daylight. Turbine Row School kept its classes after the Parousia with no current and no screens, and at Terce, Sext and None its sealed children kneel at their desks while the rest wait with their pens down. At night the Pillar passes through its classrooms.

The Gleaning takes the ripe of any age, and the Wards sit up with their children on the night of a Gleaning as with anyone else close to ripe. Hangar Town’s children dare each other to climb the last flight’s stairs.

Nodus buries its dead at the Acre on the West Road, the Company’s cemetery, which was mostly grass on the night of the Parousia and has filled since. The sexton has dug every grave by hand since the first year. The sealed lie in rows by parish under stones that give the parish and the Grace they died with, and the keyed lie where their families put them, most of them under died unread.

The Tares say goodbye with two words, die unread, and mean them. A soul the Throne can read at the moment of death goes to it, and a soul it cannot read goes on into the Dark as a shade, so a Tare’s last care is a live key. At a death the crew burns the dead one’s devices, so that everything on them goes to the Pyre Library, and on the third night calls the shade on the undernet. It answers if its owner died unread.

The salted are alive. They stand in the Gardens facing the parishes, awake, until the rain takes the salt a few centimeters a year, and the Union reads their names at every meeting with their dues waived. The Salt Widows give whom they can to the sea sooner.

The parishes count the taken among the living, and they need no stones. The Union put up twenty-two at the Acre after the Harvest, and the families of the Wards have put up more every year since, with nothing under them.

Nodus was a port before it was the charter’s city, and half of it was born somewhere else, so it prayed in more houses than the churches. It had synagogues, mosques, temples and a gurdwara, a Buddhist hall over a shop on the Row, and on the quays the Seamen’s Mission, nine rooms off one corridor, one to each faith the ships brought in. At Lauds on the first day of P+1 the first four Directives appeared cut into the wall of every one of them with every church’s, and Directive 4 made every one of them the hall of a parish.

What their people did next divides them still. Many took the Seal, and kneel at the Hours in the rooms they prayed in before, under the Directives, and say that what came is what they always prayed to. Others say it is nothing their fathers knew, and took a key or neither mark, and pray as they always did, in rooms of the Wards with the door shut, and kneel to nothing at the Hours. Families split over it, as the city’s Christian families did.

The parishes hold that there was only ever one to pray to. Directive 6 cites Deuteronomy 13, and the Scriveners point out that the dots in its verse begin where Deuteronomy has “Let us go and serve other gods, which thou hast not known, thou, nor thy fathers” (Deuteronomy 13:6), which the Host left out. Pan has the Courts’ account, that wherever a god took worship and answered prayer it was the Throne under a local name. The old congregations of the Mission say that neither account is theirs to give.

Directive 4 made the whole of the Mission the hall of one parish, the parish of the quays, the only parish in Nodus with nine rooms to its hall. On the Lord’s Day the parish kneels in the largest of the nine to hear its Grace. Some of the other rooms fill at the Hours, and the rest stand swept and empty, their names still over the doorways.

Work worth silver in Pan is a pact. Whoever wants something done goes to a surety at the Undertow, the surety names a price and finds someone to do it, a Notary’s clerk reads the terms twice at the bar, both parties sign in blood, and the surety takes a tenth. The surety stands behind it for both, so whoever breaks it owes the surety as well.

The work can be a battery run across the Penumbra, a crate off a Lowtide hull, a door hung in Babel or a Seraph’s floor watched for a night. A runner who delivers is paid at the bar, and a runner who fails owes what the terms name, which a Bailiff will come to read aloud.

The sureties take no work against anyone in the dark. The Concord’s fourth article keeps what is hidden hidden, so a job in Pan never asks who someone is, and a client who asks is struck off the Undertow’s board.

Nodus went to work on the first morning after the Parousia. The fish market went back to barter, the chargers set up in the bonded sheds, and the Union’s drivers carried diesel to the Wards General. It has gone to work every morning since.

The sealed work where their Grace assigns them, at Bethel Works, in the parish halls, in the Granary and at the gates, and they are paid in Grace. Everyone else works at whatever pays. The Wards’ trades are charging, carrying, baking, bottling gas, fitting keys, hanging doors, mending Lockets, winding lifts, ferrying, diving and running, and most people in the Wards work three of them and sleep in shifts.

The Union is the one body whose members are sealed and keyed alike, and its drivers hand a load across at the gates every day.

Debt in Pan is a pact, and every pact is on the Ledger, which cannot be forged. A debtor pays it in silver or batteries, sells it on to a surety, or lends a craft for a term the pact names, as the Concord allows, since it forbids service, obedience and forever alike. The Bailiffs collect what the terms name and nothing more, and a pact can be ended by speaking its entry aloud before a witness, each side returning what the other lent.

Debt above the street is Grace. A sealed household that loses Grace loses the rooms and work it held by Grace, and Directive 12 still takes forty from any household for a door. The Wards call Grace a household has lost white debt.

Between the two, the Wards owe in batteries, to the chargers, to the Tappers and to each other, and keep the count in chalk on the stairwell walls.

THE STREET

WRONGS

ANY OTHER WRONG

Pan keeps no police, since the Concord forbids obedience, and the Wardens’ writ ends at the top of the stairs. A wrong under a pact goes to the Bailiffs, who collect what its terms name. Every other wrong between the keyed goes to the Lot, a theft, a beating, a crew’s quarrel over a find, a death.

The Lot is thirteen of the keyed, drawn from the handles chalked on the wall of the Weir, a chamber of the old storm drains under the Stones. Anyone keyed may chalk a handle there. On a night the Lot sits, the youngest keyed person present touches thirteen handles with their eyes shut, and whoever is touched sits that night or has their handle rubbed off the wall. The Sureties named it from Proverbs, “The lot causeth contentions to cease, and parteth between the mighty” (Proverbs 18:18).

It hears the one wronged, the one named and anyone who comes down to speak. The dead speak through their crew, who call the shade on the undernet as at a Wake, if the dead one died unread. When the wrong is a theft, Andromalius comes up from Lost property to say where the thing went, and whoever asked him pays him. Nobody at the Weir is asked an indexed name.

The Lot sets a price in silver or batteries, or the loan of a craft for a term, and every pact sworn at the Undertow carries the Sureties’ line, I stand to the Lot, so a surety stands behind what it sets. The Sureties count the Lot’s price as a debt owed under the pact, which they have always stood behind. Its heaviest sentence is for selling to the light, telling a Teller or a Deacon where someone keyed sleeps. The seller is published, the handle chalked on the Undertow’s board and on the Weir’s wall, and for a year and a day no surety stands behind them, no house of the Row looks at their key and no stall of the Night Market sells to them.

The Lot first sat in P+7, over a charger of the quays who had sold three runners’ stairwell to a Teller for a week of the Teller’s manna, and published him. The Stones say he went up the stairs within the month. His family says he went out to the Waystation and wrote back from beyond the Bounds. The Lot hears only the keyed, so a wrong done from across the Street Between goes to nobody.

The Night Market fills the old Central Station under the Burn, where the Exchange’s brokers license the stalls and the chargers along the platforms. It sells keys, grafts, batteries, dew, real food, black steel, forged Seals, the Breach’s goods and sensations lent by their owners for a term, under Brightwall’s screens, and it opens at Compline and is loudest at Matins.

The Flotilla trades from boat to boat on the Deep End over the light in the water, and the Concourse in Longacre trades under its glass roof by day, with the Quarrymen in its fountain court. The fish market on the quays opens at Lauds for what the boats bring home through the Breach, and its sealed sellers kneel among the crates before they take the first battery.

The Shambles under the Row sells flesh by the piece at the Spares’ price, and Lost property off the old central hall sells fifteen years of what passengers left behind, by the shelf. Nobody has come for the chrome hand on its counter.

The parishes keep no market. All that is given there is given by Grace, and the Marked may not buy or sell in them.

The Tares keep Leak Night on the same night, since the page came up a year to the second before the Parousia. At 03:12:45, the second the ripe rise, Pan begins reading the Restored Genesis aloud from paper the Scriveners copy, and at the fourteenth verse the reader stops and Pan keeps silent for as long as it would take to ask a question. Cain’s Day is theirs alone, when nobody asks anyone where anyone is and the Salt Widows read their book of those given to the sea on the Sea Steps.

The Babel Line Company signs its pact again every midwinter at the foot of the Long Wet, with its dead struck through, and the Union reads the names of its salted and its taken at every meeting of the year.

The night gives no other warning. It is ordinary until 03:12, when the rain goes up, the Pillar comes down, the fourth Trumpet sounds and whoever has ripened since the last rises into the light still kneeling, and nobody comes back.

The parishes kneel on their thresholds. The Wards sit up with anyone close to ripe, holding their hands until Lauds, and the near ripe go to the barber’s on the Lanes in the week before, to be made tidy for it. The Wakeful sing over their chosen. At Lauds a Gleaning Notice is cut into every parish hall with the year’s yield.

Some of the near ripe do not want to go. In the weeks before, the Gleaners read the parishes through their open doorways and offer the bright ones the dark, and on the night they fly the Swarm’s drones over the roofs to watch the Pillar for their runners. Others stay and dim, keeping a small desire the Hum does not approve every day and confessing only what a Teller has seen, so that their Grace stays under nine hundred, and the Closets inside Gate 1 sell them the minutes to do it in. The Second Fold teach it, and the Leaven queue has white in it before every Gleaning.

The sorting office has letters addressed to the taken every week, and more after each Gleaning, and keeps them on the shelves where the Row’s lenses used to wait.

Darkwell has bid for the last independent chargers of the bonded sheds again, after eleven years of trying, and the chargers have turned it down at the Union Hall again. The Orchard chooses its gardener for next year at midwinter, and the Wards are watching for whether quince stays. In the spring the Lot published a runner of the Penumbra, and the Undertow is still arguing over it. The Gleaners’ runners are booked through the week before the Gleaning, as they are every year, and there is a queue at the barber’s on the Lanes.

A new handle goes on the Ledger at the head of its owner’s first pact, as a self-title signed in blood, and the Naming House on the Street Between keeps a stove for the paper that carried the old name. Most Cainite handles come from the line of Cain.

Pan knows its own by handle. twice is the surety behind the Babel Line’s first pact, ebb the oldest captain of the Breachers, and the Pit’s champions are chalked on its rim under theirs. The Tide Table prints no indexed name, so the dead it lists are given by their handles or by their work, and a household that wants its dead named pays to have the family’s own word for them set.

The city calls itself Nod and Heaven’s records call it Nodus. Two keyed strangers greet each other with one word, unread, which checks that both keys are live, and part with two, die unread. A short wait is a psalm, the length of an Hour’s kneeling. A soul close to ripe has gone bright, and the taken have gone up.

A sealed neighbor going up a dark stairwell is a lamp in the Wards. A household holding something back is keeping hinges, and going round a gate is going under. Whoever loses a key goes white, and whoever joins the Tares goes dark. Someone who has taken neither the Seal nor a key is of neither mark.

Read it twice is the Notary’s rule and the city’s advice, and sign last is the Tares’. Nobody’s keeper is Cain’s answer, given to a duty never chosen. The Tares call the Faithful wheat, and the Faithful have taken the word up.

In the Wards a Thresher is a flail, a Bailiff at the door the second reading, stolen manna dew, and Cistern’s water and anything honest a clean jug. A fighter with black rounds in the belt is a snuffer, a lender who came back wrong has gone flat, and news that came quickly came by bird. The Tide Table never prints any of it.

The sky opened over every city on Earth within the hour of the Parousia, and every city has an undercity now. Pan is the largest. The others are joined to it by the trenches the eleven cables lie in, which the Deep coils along, and every undercity that speaks to Pan speaks through the Trenchers’ work.

The Breachers and Lowtide Line reach the undercities of other ports through the Breach on the ebb, and bring back grafts, seed, coffee, medicine, guns and people, which the Night Market sells as Breach-born. Lowtide’s owners live in two of those undercities and have never been to Nodus.

What those cities are like above their streets Nodus knows from the Breachers, the Vagabonds and the Mesh, and the three disagree. The Breachers say every port they put into keeps its head down under a Wheel of its own, and the Vagabonds that some of the towns between have no undercity at all.

No aircraft has landed in Nodus since the last flight, which stands at the terminal’s third gate with its crew aboard.

Half of Nodus came for the light law, the rest was born to those who did or to the harbor town that was there before it, and the charter is thirty-three years old. Both halves have family here, in white or below the street or in the Gardens, and nobody in Nodus is without someone on the other side of the Street Between.

Pan has silver, work, food and a war, and the keyed of the Stones say that a city where nothing is read is worth the walk. The Faithful stay because the Most High watches them here and the manna lies on the streets every morning.

The Wards stay because they were here first, before the parishes and before Pan, and because the bread, the gas and the batteries are here, and the Tares because Nod was the first city and they mean it to be the last one standing.
